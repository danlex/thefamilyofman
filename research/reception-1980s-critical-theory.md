# 1980s photography-theory reception — perspective note

This note accompanies the bench of 1980s photography-theory entries added
in PR #92 (Phillips 1982 "Judgment Seat", Burgin 1982, Crimp 1980/1987/1993,
Foster 1983/1985/1988, Krauss 1981/1985, Rosler 1981, Sekula 1983/1986/1995,
Tagg 1988). It extends — and should be read alongside —
`research/reception-1970s-critical-theory.md`.

The 1980s Anglo-American critical-theory turn documented in this bench is
one strand of *Family of Man* reception, not the whole picture. Three
qualifications belong alongside it.

## 1. Coexistence, not supersession

While Phillips, Sekula, Crimp, Krauss, and Tagg were dismantling MoMA's
curatorial premises in the pages of *October*, *Screen*, and Bay Press
volumes, the Luxembourg copy of *The Family of Man* was on partial display
at Clervaux Castle (1975–1989) and the catalog remained in print. The
academic-discourse shift co-existed with — it did not supersede — the
exhibition's institutional and popular life. Phrasing in the 1980s source
notes that frames Phillips's reading as "the reason why" FoM must be
understood, or that uses past tense for "the Steichen era", describes the
critical-theory consensus, not the exhibition's continued reception.

## 2. The Phillips 1982 entry is one influential reading, not settled fact

Christopher Phillips's "The Judgment Seat of Photography" (*October* 22)
is the canonical *October*-axis critique of MoMA photography under Steichen
and Szarkowski, and is correctly the centerpiece of this batch. It is not,
however, the only scholarly framing of FoM's institutional context. The
historical-contextual strand (Sandeen 1995), the Cold War-cultural-diplomacy
strand (Turner 2013, Stimson 2006), and the continuing-popular-reception
strand (Clervaux/UNESCO/CNA programming) read the same institutional
apparatus through different theoretical lenses and reach different
verdicts. Phillips's reading is influential and should be cited; it should
not be treated as the only available reading of MoMA's "judgment seat".

## 3. Anglophone bias persists in this batch

The Bourdieu / Flusser / Eco gap flagged by PR #91's 1970s perspective
note is not closed in this batch. Of 15 entries: 14 are US-based and one
is UK (Burgin). The major non-Anglophone 1980s voices on photography
remain absent:

- **Pierre Bourdieu**, *Un art moyen* (1965; English 1990) — class-cultural
  account of photography's social uses, directly relevant to FoM's mass
  popular reception.
- **Vilém Flusser**, *Für eine Philosophie der Fotografie* (1983; English
  2000) — phenomenological/communicational theory of the photograph.
- **Umberto Eco's** photography essays in *Faith in Fakes / Travels in
  Hyperreality* (1973–1986).
- **Gisèle Freund**, *Photographie et société* (1974) — historical-
  sociological account in a humanist-documentary lineage.

The batch's stated scope (Anglo-American Sekula-era / *October*-axis
scholarship) explains the selection but does not justify silently
foregoing the counter-perspective. Future batches should address this gap.

## 4. 1980s sympathetic-to-humanist-documentary voices not represented

The batch contains no 1980s defender of the concerned-photography or
humanist-documentary tradition. Voices not represented include:

- **Robert Adams**, *Beauty in Photography* (Aperture, 1981) — defence of
  the documentary aesthetic against the *October* critique.
- **Cornell Capa** and the ICP / *Concerned Photographer* tradition
  (1968 forward, active through the 1980s).
- **John Szarkowski**'s own published writings — the named target of
  Phillips 1982, but a primary source for the curatorial position
  Phillips opposes; *Looking at Photographs* (1973) and the MoMA
  catalogue essays are still in circulation.

These omissions reflect the batch's design as critical-theory bench
rather than complete coverage; flagging them here lets a museum reader
see the shape of what is, and is not, in the bibliography.
