# 2020s reception: the 70th anniversary, the FoMLEG project, and the AI-era humanism critique

> **A note on the framing "2020s reception".** This essay covers reception
> from 2020 through May 2026 — slightly more than half the decade. Its
> centre of gravity falls in 2024–2026, when three institutional events
> converged: the launch of the FoMLEG research programme at the University
> of Luxembourg's C²DH (October 2024); the 70th-anniversary observances
> built around the 24 January 2025 anniversary date and the 24 May 2025
> Clervaux symposium; and the conservation-and-governance crisis at CNA
> Clervaux that broke into the Luxembourgish press in February–April 2026.
> The essay does not yet have access to the peer-reviewed proceedings of
> the 70th-anniversary symposium, nor to the FoMLEG dissertations and
> published outputs scheduled for later in the decade; those belong in a
> future pass. The arguments quoted at the page level are limited to
> sources retrieved by direct curl fetch on 2026-05-09 (see
> `fresh_fetches_this_round`).

This essay accompanies the 2020s bench of source entries
(`sources/2020s/`) and continues the decade-by-decade reception thread
established in `research/reception-1950s-us-press.md` through
`research/reception-2010s-turner-era.md`. It is to be read alongside
`research/clervaux.md` (custodial history at the CNA, including the
2010–2013 restoration whose end-state is the conservation envelope
breached in 2025), `research/world-tour.md` (the venue-by-venue tour
record, which the FoMLEG project is methodologically dedicated to
rewriting), and `research/africa-gap.md` (USIA Africa programme and its
relationship to the 1958 Johannesburg stop, the 2020s strand most
heavily worked by Newbury).

## 1. The decade's three institutional movements

The 2020s reception is shaped by three simultaneous but distinct
movements.

First, **the scholarly consolidation** continues the 2010s expansion
of geographic frame. The most concentrated 2020s contribution is
Darren Newbury's twin output on USIA-Africa: the Penn State
University Press monograph *Cold War Photographic Diplomacy: The US
Information Agency and Africa* (2024)
[src-newbury-2024-cold-war-photographic-diplomacy], with a companion *History of Photography*
journal article in the same year (47:3, pp. 269–297)
[src-newbury-2024-african-looks-at-america], and the prior 2023
chapter on Ernest Cole in Phu/Duganne/Noble's edited Duke UP volume
*Cold War Camera* [src-newbury-2023-cole-cold-war-camera]. To this
strand belongs Yumi Kim Takenaka's 2022 chapter in Bloomsbury's
*Capture Japan* on the 1956–57 Japan tour and the Yamahata
atomic-bomb removal controversy [src-takenaka-2022-atomic-bombings].

Second, **the institutional digital-humanities turn** is announced
by the launch of FoMLEG at the C²DH (Luxembourg Centre for
Contemporary and Digital History) in October 2024 under PI Andreas
Fickers, with Claude Ewert and Emilia Sánchez González as lead
researchers [src-c2dh-fomleg, src-c2dh-fomleg-lasting-legacy,
src-c2dh-fomleg-world-tour]. FoMLEG is the first multi-year
institutionally funded project specifically dedicated to the
exhibition's reception history. Its methodological framing is
"glocal," its explicit theoretical apparatus combines
"visual studies, decolonial studies, intergenerational memory
production, translation and reception studies, and transnational
media history" (FoMLEG project page, fetched 2026-05-09), and its
deliverables include a deep-mapping interactive website, a graphic
novel, and a PhD dissertation. The project's archival fieldwork
phase began in January 2025 with visits by Sánchez González to the
George Eastman Museum, the MoMA Archives, and the U.S. National
Archives — the first NARA RG 306 access reported in any of the
project's referenced sources (see `research/africa-gap.md`).

Third, **the AI-era critical reframing** appears in a single but
substantial 2024 contribution: Helen Lewandowski, "Zombie Humanism,
Generative AI Images, and Photography," *Panorama: Journal of the
Association of Historians of American Art* 10, no. 2 (Fall 2024),
DOI 10.24926/24716839.19237 [src-lewandowski-2024-zombie-humanism].
The article — formally a review of the Flickr Foundation's *A
Generated Family of Man* (2023) — extends the Barthesian critical
tradition into the post-photographic, generative-AI image-production
context.

To these three sit beside (i) a popular-press 70th-anniversary
cluster of August–October 2025 features
[src-dench-2025-amateur-photographer-fom,
src-george-2025-digital-camera-world-fom,
src-west-2025-dailyart-lange-fom], (ii) the obituary of one of the
last living photographers in the show (Frank Horvat, 1928–2020)
[src-nyt-2020-horvat-obit], (iii) a Luxembourg-press lecture report
on Steichen's connection to the Grand Duchy
[src-chronicle-lu-2025-cercle-cite-steichen], and (iv) the 2026
woxx investigative pair on the Clervaux conservation crisis and the
resignation of CNA Director Gilles Zeimet
[src-woxx-2026-fom-kulturpolitischer-problemfall].

This essay treats those three movements in turn (§§3–5) after a
brief recap of the prior critical canon (§2) and closes with the
2026 conservation-and-governance crisis (§6) and a synthesis of
where the 2020s reading sits relative to earlier decades (§7).

## 2. The pre-2020 horizon, briefly

The reception thread carries the following positions into the 2020s.

- **Roland Barthes (1957 *Mythologies*)** — humanism as
  myth-making, naturalising the historical and political conditions
  of the "human family" [discussed in
  `research/reception-1970s-critical-theory.md`; not re-read this
  round].
- **Susan Sontag 1977, Allan Sekula 1981, Christopher Phillips
  1982** — the *October*-axis political critique consolidating
  Barthes into a broader argument about MoMA photography and
  US Cold War cultural diplomacy [discussed in
  `research/reception-1980s-critical-theory.md`; not re-read this
  round].
- **Eric Sandeen 1995** — the historical-contextual book-length
  reconstruction of the exhibition as Cold War cultural diplomacy
  [discussed in `research/reception-1990s-clervaux-installation.md`].
- **Blake Stimson 2006**, *The Pivot of the World* — a sympathetic
  but qualified reading of FoM as one term in a comparative
  argument about mass-photographic objects and national
  self-understanding [discussed in
  `research/reception-2000s-stimson-era.md`].
- **Fred Turner 2012/2013** — the most sustained scholarly defence
  of the show's intentional design and audience invitation, framing
  the exhibition's installation logic as anti-fascist Bauhaus
  inheritance via Bayer 1942 [discussed in
  `research/reception-2010s-turner-era.md`; Turner 2012 *Public
  Culture* 24:1 read in full in the 2010s round but not re-read this
  round].
- **Hurm/Reitz/Zamir (eds.) 2018**, *The Family of Man Revisited*
  — the major late-2010s essay collection, framed by its editors
  as "challenging in particular the legacy of Roland Barthes's
  influential account" [CNA documentation, fetched in the 2010s
  round; not re-read this round].

The 2020s do not collapse these. They add three new registers
without retiring any.

## 3. The scholarly consolidation: Newbury, Takenaka, the post-2018 essay collection silence

The 2020s' most concentrated peer-reviewed scholarly output on
*Family of Man*'s reception comes from Darren Newbury, Professor of
Photographic History at the University of Brighton. Three Newbury
publications belong in this decade's bench.

**Newbury 2023, Cole chapter in *Cold War Camera* (Duke UP)**
[src-newbury-2023-cole-cold-war-camera]. Chapter title: "Ernest
Cole's *House of Bondage*, the United States Information Agency,
and the Cultural Politics of Race in the Cold War." Pages 33–65.
The chapter's central argument is structurally parallel to the
*October*-axis 1980s critique of FoM, but transposed to a
counter-archive: where Sekula 1981 and Phillips 1982 read FoM
from inside the institutional apparatus that produced it, Newbury
2023 reads Ernest Cole's *House of Bondage* (1967) as the
counter-evidence the same apparatus could not contain. The Duke UP
chapter-abstract page returned 403 on direct fetch 2026-05-09 and
2026-04-30; Cole-chapter body text is **not** consulted at the page
level in this project. The argument is described here from
metadata only.

**Newbury 2024 monograph, *Cold War Photographic Diplomacy: The US
Information Agency and Africa*** (Penn State UP)
[src-newbury-2024-cold-war-photographic-diplomacy]. The PSU Press
publisher page was fetched 2026-05-09 and the description, table
of contents, and editorial reviews are now confirmed verbatim. The
publisher's description (verbatim, fetched 2026-05-09) sets up the
book's argument as follows:

> "The emergence of newly independent African nations onto the
> world stage in the mid-twentieth century precipitated a contest
> for influence among Cold War superpowers, leading the United
> States to mount an international campaign of photographic
> diplomacy underpinned by a faith in the medium's capacity to
> cross cultural boundaries. However, the increasing global
> visibility of racial injustice undermined US claims that the
> nation had transcended colonial racism." (PSU Press, fetched
> 2026-05-09)

> "Drawing on extensive research in the archives of the United
> States Information Agency (USIA) and concentrating on the period
> from the mid-1950s through to the late 1960s, Darren Newbury
> traces the role of photography in the United States' appeal to
> Africa." (PSU Press, fetched 2026-05-09)

Two observations belong on the reception thread.

First, the PSU Press description itself does not name *The Family
of Man* or Steichen. The book is sold and framed on its own terms:
USIA photographic diplomacy in Africa, 1955–late-1960s. Earlier
versions of the project's source-file for Newbury 2024 carried a
quotation beginning *"The global tour of Edward Steichen's 1955
photographic exhibition The Family of Man, sponsored by the USIA,
has long been viewed as the defining moment…"* — that passage was
provisionally attributed to the publisher blurb on the basis of a
WebSearch result. **It is not on the PSU Press page as fetched
2026-05-09.** The earlier note's caution that the passage might be
AI paraphrase rather than verbatim publisher text was correct, and
the passage has been removed from the source file
[src-newbury-2024-cold-war-photographic-diplomacy] in this round.
The framing "FoM as the visible tip of a much larger USIA
photographic apparatus" is a research synthesis attributable to
this project, not to Newbury or PSU Press.

Second, the table of contents (verbatim from the PSU Press page,
fetched 2026-05-09) is now confirmed:

> "1. Photography, Race, and the Cold War Imagination / 2.
> Photography, Public Diplomacy, and the Africa Program at the
> United States Information Agency / 3. 'Toward a Better World':
> Africa, the United Nations, and the Photographic Diplomacy of
> Decolonization / 4. 'A Pleasant Mixture of Negro and White':
> Photographing Civil Rights as Democracy in Action / 5. 'Africans
> at the Wax Museum': Photography and International Friendship /
> 6. 'Don't Touch Those Windows': United States Information
> Service Exhibits in Africa / Epilogue / Appendix: Archival
> Series Abbreviations / Notes / Bibliography / Index." (PSU
> Press, fetched 2026-05-09)

The chapter body text was **not** retrieved in this round. The
PSU Press page links a free PDF preface
(`/sample_chapter/Newbury_Preface.pdf`) which was not fetched. No
page-level citations to Newbury 2024 are made in this essay.

**Newbury 2024 article, "An African Looks at America: Picturing
Racial Integration for Africa, 1956–68," *History of Photography*
47:3, pp. 269–297, DOI 10.1080/03087298.2024.2323310**
[src-newbury-2024-african-looks-at-america]. Tandfonline returned
403 on direct fetch 2026-05-09; the abstract page was not
retrieved. Body text is paywalled. The article is described in
this essay only at the level of bibliographic metadata and the
prior `src-` file's search-snippet entry; no verbatim text is
quoted.

**Takenaka 2022, "Shadows of the Atomic Bombings in *The Family
of Man*: The American Photographic Exhibition Tour of Japan in
the Post-Occupation Period," in *Capture Japan* (Bloomsbury
Academic), DOI 10.5040/9781350186811.0021**
[src-takenaka-2022-atomic-bombings]. Bloomsbury Collections
returned a generic landing-shell page on direct fetch 2026-05-09
(no chapter content). The chapter body is not consulted at the
page level in this project. The pre-existing source-file entry
records a search-snippet verbatim, dated 2026-04-30, of what
appears to be the chapter's opening framing:

> "The exhibition attracted one million visitors in Japan and the
> immediate removal of some photographs of the atomic bombing on
> Nagasaki, which were specially added to the installation, caused
> controversy. This characterized the reception of *The Family of
> Man* in Japan as being in cultural tensions between the
> aspirations for nuclear energy and the fears of nuclear disaster
> in the 1950s." (search-result excerpt, recorded 2026-04-30 in
> [src-takenaka-2022-atomic-bombings]; not re-fetched 2026-05-09)

Takenaka's chapter is the most substantial English-language
scholarly account of the Tokyo / 1956–57 Japan tour, and the
Yamahata photographs / *Asahi Graph* April 1956 protest are the
clearest case in the existing literature where a host country's
audience-press response materially altered the exhibition's
content. The chapter belongs in the 2020s bench as a venue-level
reception study; the page-level argument awaits a future pass with
library access to the Bloomsbury volume.

**A reception-thread observation about the post-2018 silence.**
Hurm/Reitz/Zamir's 2018 *Family of Man Revisited* (I.B. Tauris),
characterised by its editors as a volume "challenging in particular
the legacy of Roland Barthes's influential account of the
exhibition" (CNA documentation page, fetched in the 2010s round),
is *not* answered by an equivalent essay collection in the 2020s.
Newbury 2024's monograph is the closest thing to a 2020s book-length
counterpart, but it is on a different scholarly object (USIA-Africa
photographic diplomacy at the institutional / archival level) and
not on FoM as such. Takenaka 2022 is a chapter, not a monograph.
Lewandowski 2024 (§5) is an article-length review of a
non-academic project. The most ambitious 2020s book-length
treatment dedicated specifically to FoM's reception remains
unwritten as of May 2026 — the FoMLEG project (§4) is the closest
candidate but had not yet produced its planned monograph or PhD
dissertation by this round's cutoff.

## 4. The institutional digital-humanities turn: FoMLEG

The single most consequential 2020s institutional event for FoM
reception scholarship is the launch of FoMLEG ("Tracing the legacy
of Edward Steichen: A glocal approach to the international
reception and national heritagisation of the 'Family of Man'
exhibition") at the C²DH (Luxembourg Centre for Contemporary and
Digital History), University of Luxembourg, in October 2024
[src-c2dh-fomleg, src-c2dh-fomleg-lasting-legacy].

The project page (datePublished 2024-04-10, dateModified
2025-10-16; project status "active"; project type "fnr —
luxembourg") was directly fetched 2026-05-09. The first paragraph
of the project description is worth quoting at length, because it
is the cleanest available statement of the project's research
question:

> "The photographic heritage linked to Edward Steichen has by now
> a permanent place in the cultural landscape of Luxembourg.
> Composed of public and private collections, this heritage is
> anchored in the history of photography internationally, in
> Luxembourg's collective memory and in various cultural
> institutions and collections in the Grand Duchy. This
> multilayered and transdisciplinary research project aims to shed
> light on hitherto underexposed subjects of these collections and
> to direct its attention towards a twofold exploration: the
> international reception of the exhibition The Family of Man
> (FoM) during its world tour between 1955 and 1964 at selected
> locations and the heritagisation of Edward Steichen's oeuvre and
> legacy in the specific cultural and political contexts of
> Luxembourg and its evolution up until today." (FoMLEG project
> page, fetched 2026-05-09)

The methodological framing — "glocal" — and the explicit
theoretical apparatus is laid out in the second paragraph:

> "This research project aims at problematizing the audience
> response to the travelling exhibition using a glocal history
> approach: The multilayered and transdisciplinary approach is
> aimed at enriching the existing scholarship on FoM by combining
> global, national, and local processes of circulation,
> translation, and appropriation of photographs in order to
> retrace and understand the itinerancy of the exhibition and its
> audience responses. […] In framing the FoM as a global media
> event, we aim to analyse the local performances of the
> exhibition as ritualised moments of thickened media
> communication, revealing specific strategies of territorial
> appropriation of globally circulating messages. The project
> emphasises the need for a critical reflection on the colonial
> and post-colonial dimension of FoM by paying more attention to
> the question of how the exhibition has been received in the
> Global South." (FoMLEG project page, fetched 2026-05-09)

Three observations belong on the reception thread.

First, the project's chosen theoretical vocabulary is explicit
about its inheritance. "Encoding/decoding" is Stuart Hall's
1973/1980 framework; "glocal history" is the term Andreas Fickers
(the project's PI) and others have developed at the C²DH for
combining global, national, and local scales of analysis in
post-1945 European media history; "decolonial studies" indicates
that the Anglophone critical tradition of Sekula and Solomon-Godeau
is being supplemented by a Latin American / Subaltern Studies
register that the *October* axis itself did not adopt. The Lewandowski
2024 essay (§5) cites Ariella Azoulay as the most prominent prior
recent figure to attempt a "critical rehabilitation" of FoM via
"global citizenry"; FoMLEG's framing carries that thread forward
methodologically without prejudging the conclusion.

Second, the project's explicit 1955–1964 dating of the world tour
(verbatim from the project page, fetched 2026-05-09) is one of
several dating windows in the 2020s sources. Lewandowski 2024's
footnote 1 (verbatim, fetched 2026-05-09) gives "until 1958" for
the U.S. tour and "until 1963" for USIA international touring —
explicitly distinguishing two phases. The C²DH "Lasting Legacy"
news article (fetched 2026-05-09) says the exhibition "toured 37
countries" and lists the regions visited (verbatim 2026-05-09):

> "Although the exhibition's world tour focused on North America,
> Europe, and East Asia (Japan and South Korea), it would go
> beyond the Iron Curtain (Poland and the USSR) and to several
> countries in the Global South (Guatemala, Chile, South Africa,
> Egypt, Afghanistan), many of which had only recently become
> independent nations (India, Sri Lanka, Indonesia, the
> Philippines, Laos, Lebanon, Syria)." (C²DH FoMLEG news article,
> fetched 2026-05-09)

The project's own data and Lewandowski's tour list overlap but are
not identical (Lewandowski's footnote 1 names "Afghanistan,
Colombia, Cuba, France, Indonesia, Iran, Israel, Kenya, Laos,
Poland, South Africa, Sweden, and Yugoslavia" as a representative
list, not a full tour itinerary). The "37 countries" figure is
endorsed by FoMLEG but, as `research/world-tour.md` §3 records, it
is unanchored to a primary archival source in any of the project's
fetched materials. FoMLEG's promised deep-mapping deliverable is
expected to settle this question; the question is open as of
2026-05-09.

Third, the January 2025 archival fieldwork phase narrated in
`src-c2dh-fomleg-world-tour` (the C²DH news article authored by
Sánchez González, dated 3 March 2025, fetched 2026-05-09) is the
first reported direct access to NARA RG 306 in any of the
project's referenced sources. The article reproduces a verbatim
caption from a NARA RG 306 photograph (306-FM-3) — Bertolt Brecht
crossing from East Berlin to visit FoM at the Hochschule für
Bildende Künste in West Berlin in 1955 (caption verbatim, fetched
2026-05-09):

> "German playwright and poet Bertolt Brecht crosses from East
> Berlin to visit 'The Family of Man' exhibition at the
> Hochschule für Bildende Künste in West Berlin, Germany. 1955.
> Records of the U.S. Information Agency. Courtesy National
> Archives, photo no. 306-FM-3." (C²DH news article, NARA RG 306
> caption reproduced verbatim, fetched 2026-05-09)

This caption is the only freshly-fetched anchor for the 1955 West
Berlin venue (Hochschule für Bildende Künste) currently in the
project's bench, and it is sourced through C²DH's institutional
voice rather than through a direct NARA fetch — RG 306 itself
remains inaccessible to this project's tooling, see
`src-nara-rg306-africa-access-barrier`. FoMLEG's institutional
significance for the 2020s reception is therefore concrete: it has
done, on Sánchez González's January 2025 fieldwork visit to
College Park, primary-archival work that secondary scholarship
(including this project) has not yet been able to reproduce.

The 24 May 2025 Clervaux symposium "70 Years of The Family of
Man" [src-c2dh-70-years-fom] organised in the FoMLEG framework
brought together (verbatim from the symposium event page, fetched
2026-05-09): Prof. Dr Gerd Hurm (TCAS, University of Trier;
keynote and recipient of the laudatio); Prof. Shamoon Zamir
(NYU/AD), "Photography and cosmopolitan citizenship: The politics
of *The Family of Man*"; Dr Claude Ewert (University of
Luxembourg); Prof. Paul Lesch (Steichen Collections, Luxembourg),
"Steichen, US camera and *The Family of Man*"; and Prof. Dr Astrid
Böger, "Robert Frank and *The Family of Man*: A double take." The
event was held at Clervaux Castle on 24 May 2025, 10:30–16:30.
Hurm and Zamir are both editors of the 2018 *Family of Man
Revisited* volume, signalling continuity between the late-2010s
essay collection and the 2024-launched institutional project.
Symposium proceedings, if published, will be Tier-2 entries in the
project's bench when they appear.

## 5. The AI-era critical reframing: Lewandowski 2024 and "zombie humanism"

Helen Lewandowski's "Zombie Humanism, Generative AI Images, and
Photography," *Panorama: Journal of the Association of Historians
of American Art* 10, no. 2 (Fall 2024), DOI 10.24926/24716839.19237
[src-lewandowski-2024-zombie-humanism], is the only identified
2020s scholarly English-language article that directly addresses
both *The Family of Man* and generative AI. The 10-page PDF was
retrieved by curl on 2026-05-09 from
https://journalpanorama.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Lewandowski-Zombie-Humanism.pdf
(cache: `.scratch/lewandowski-2024-zombie-humanism.pdf`) and read
in full this round. All quotations below are verbatim from that
retrieved PDF.

The article is formally a review of the Flickr Foundation's *A
Generated Family of Man* (2023; archived at
https://archive.org/details/a-generated-family-of-man), the third
volume in the Foundation's "A Flickr of Humanity" New Curators
Programme. *A Generated Family of Man* was produced over summer
2023 by Goldsmiths design students Juwon Jung and Maya Osaka, who
attempted to recreate around half of the photographs in *The
Family of Man* catalogue using Microsoft Bing's Image Creator
(DALL-E backed). Two prompting methods were used: human prompts
written by the team, and "AI prompts" generated by feeding
screenshots of original FoM photographs into an Image-to-Caption
model (Lewandowski 2024, p. 2).

Lewandowski's opening paragraph is the cleanest single-paragraph
summary of the prior critical reception in the 2020s sources
bench, and is worth quoting at length:

> "Critique of *The Family of Man* and its themes seems to return
> like clockwork every few years—each endeavoring to offer new
> insight on the subject. […] In 1956, Roland Barthes appraised
> this type of humanist photography as a conservative abstraction
> that naturalized insidious elements of this 'family'; in 1981,
> Allan Sekula dissected 'the oft-repeated claim that photography
> constitutes a "universal language"'; in 2004, Abigail
> Solomon-Godeau viewed its permanent display as 'a kind of
> Benetton ad' with advertising co-opting this 'representational
> work'; in the 2010s, Ariella Azoulay provided a critical
> rehabilitation of the exhibition, arguing that it should be
> understood by its multiplicity and global citizenry. The list
> goes on." (Lewandowski 2024, p. 1)

(Note on Barthes dating: Lewandowski writes "1956," which refers
to the original publication of Barthes's "La grande famille des
hommes" in *Les Lettres nouvelles* in 1956, ahead of the
collected *Mythologies* in 1957. Lewandowski's footnote 2 cites
Barthes via the Hill and Wang/Howard 2012 *Mythologies* edition,
pp. 196–99.)

The article's central thesis is announced on p. 6, where
Lewandowski coins the diagnostic phrase "zombie humanism" and
positions it relative to the broader 21st-century humanist
resurgence she had identified in her 2023 Courtauld dissertation
[Lewandowski 2024, p. 6 with footnote 31 citing the dissertation]:

> "Twenty-first-century humanism has focused on neoliberal
> individualism, subjectivity, and first-person narratives,
> emphasizing the voices of both subject and author. While this
> humanist resurgence may be criticized as merely gestural, the
> subsequent images produced by programs like DALL-E and
> Midjourney artificially reanimate this digitally
> overrepresented 2010s culture, a mixture of scraped
> photographs from search engines, social-media platforms, and
> stock photography. This is what I would term 'zombie humanism,'
> incidentally producing the same commercially motivated
> 'everyday' tropes but presenting them untethered from
> historical, political, or photographic contexts (a
> nineteenth-century Alice Liddell revivified through Tumblr; a
> 1940s dead soldier transmorphed into a 2010s influencer)."
> (Lewandowski 2024, p. 6)

The thesis has two structural moves. First, it reads *A Generated
Family of Man* — and by extension the broader generative-AI image
practice it represents — as a continuation rather than a
displacement of the humanist visual programme Steichen instituted
in 1955. The Flickr Foundation project "hollow[s] out *The Family
of Man* to its form alone" (p. 1) by inserting AI-generated images
into the catalogue's recognisable layout. Second, it reads the
photographic-ontological collapse implicit in that gesture as a
specifically harmful version of postwar humanism, not an exit
from it.

The *Family of Man*-relevant payoff comes on p. 7 of the article,
in two places. First, Lewandowski engages directly with Barthes
on the concept of *punctum* in the *A Generated Family of Man*
prologue:

> "A reference to Roland Barthes's *punctum* in the prologue by
> Fattori McKenna is critical of generative AI's hollow imagery
> but is ultimately misapplied. It is not that there is some
> elusive, specific aesthetic detail in a photograph that causes
> a personal, emotional 'prick' to the viewer (not all, or many,
> photographs will have a *punctum* for the viewer). It is the
> fact that these images are not photographs; there is no
> *punctum* or even *studium* (a detached political, cultural, or
> historical interest in a photograph). Generative AI images may
> aspire to visually resemble photographs, but crucially they
> have no medium-specific relationship to reality ('the
> Photograph is pure contingency and can be nothing else,' in
> the Barthesian view). Barthes's 1967 essay 'Death of the
> Author' might be a more relevant though flippant reference;
> the catalogue's afterword (a reprinted article from *Lawfare*,
> not written for the project) by Flickr's board chair Ryan
> Merkley repeats the argument continually made by tech companies
> that AI is a 'transformative use' of materials, which elude the
> author's ownership." (Lewandowski 2024, p. 7)

Second, the article's closing paragraph (p. 7) returns explicitly
to the 1956 Barthes critique and treats generative AI's effect on
*Family of Man*-style humanism as a *naturalisation* of the same
biases Barthes had originally identified — but worse:

> "Rather than a Braidottian understanding of posthumanism,
> liberated from the conservative and anthropocentric humanism
> associated with postwar humanist photography, there is instead
> a risk of further entrenching harmful humanist biases, reifying
> what people want to see or believe. This is neither a return to
> the 'old humanist institutions that created the conditions for
> greater equality,' which Stimson reminisced about, nor
> Azoulay's reformed humanism, which rejects postwar humanism's
> implicit white colonial man in favor of the heroic figure of the
> citizen. Instead, generative AI's zombie humanism is, at best,
> as per Barthes's 1956 text, a further *naturalization* of
> ingrained human biases. At worst, however, it inhabits the
> visual signifiers of humanism while actively discrediting the
> photojournalistic corpus that documents our shared human
> history." (Lewandowski 2024, p. 7)

Three observations belong on the reception thread.

First, Lewandowski's own framing is explicitly Barthesian. The
phrase "naturalization" is the term Barthes used in the 1957
*Mythologies* essay to describe what humanist photography did to
historical contingency; Lewandowski applies the same vocabulary to
2023-era generative-AI image production. This is, on Barthes's
own grammar, a *worse* version of the same gesture: where
humanist photography in 1955 naturalised historical conditions of
postwar life, 2023 generative-AI images naturalise the
already-naturalised — they reinforce the bias structures of
2010s commercial photography, which were themselves the inheritance
of a longer humanist visual programme. The Barthesian critique
of FoM is, in Lewandowski's reading, more relevant to *A Generated
Family of Man* (2023) than it was to *The Family of Man* (1955).

Second, the article does explicit work positioning Lewandowski's
"zombie humanism" against the prior 2010s recovery readings
— Stimson 2006 ("the 'old humanist institutions that created the
conditions for greater equality'" — Lewandowski cites this via a
2013 Stimson web essay, not via *The Pivot of the World*) and
Azoulay's "reformed humanism" — and also against the broader
posthumanist register represented by Rosi Braidotti. Notably,
Turner's name does not appear in the article's body text or
footnotes. Lewandowski's argument runs through the
Barthes/Sekula/Solomon-Godeau/Azoulay/Stimson axis, not through
the Turner / Bauhaus-installation-design defence. This is
characteristic of the 2020s critical landscape: Turner's 2012
*Public Culture* article and 2013 *Democratic Surround* chapter
have not produced an interlocutor at the same level on the
contrary side. Lewandowski's intervention is in the
Barthes-axis tradition.

Third, the article's empirical content — its direct engagement
with *A Generated Family of Man* — produces specific observations
about what AI-image-generation does to particular FoM images that
have no equivalent in earlier critical literature. Lewandowski
notes (p. 4) that the Warsaw Ghetto 1943 image (the only FoM
image directly related to the Holocaust, on Lewandowski's reading)
was "thankfully only represented through a human prompt and not an
AI one" in *A Generated Family of Man*; that Raphael Platnick's
1944 *Eniwetok* photograph of a dead U.S. soldier was AI-rendered
as "a lifestyle magazine-type image of a blonde woman against a
field, captioned: 'Taking a moment to connect with nature's
embrace #FieldOfDreams #GroundedInTheWilderness #SerenityInTheGrass'"
(Lewandowski 2024, p. 5); and that Dorothea Lange's 1935 FSA
image of Filipino farm workers was misread by the AI as showing
elephants (with the AI-prompt caption "Behold the mighty elephants,
creating beautiful footprints in the soft earth") (p. 3).

These observations are not, in themselves, generalisable
arguments about FoM's reception. They are the empirical core of
why Lewandowski reads *A Generated Family of Man* as a particularly
acute case of the broader generative-AI cultural-memory problem —
the show that committed itself most thoroughly to a humanist
visual programme is the one whose AI-regenerated form makes the
collapse of that programme into commercial-stock-photography
clichés most legible.

## 6. The 2026 conservation-and-governance crisis at Clervaux

Two *woxx* articles in April 2026 mark the most institutionally
fraught episode in the 2020s for the Luxembourgish custody of the
exhibition. Both articles were directly fetched 2026-05-09 (caches:
`.scratch/woxx-2026-fom-kulturpolitischer-problemfall.html` and
`.scratch/woxx-2026-fom-demission-und-diskussionen.html`). All
quotations below are verbatim from those retrieved HTML files.

The lead article — Chris Lauer, "*The Family of Man*:
Kulturpolitischer Problemfall," *woxx* 02.04.2026
[src-woxx-2026-fom-kulturpolitischer-problemfall] — opens on the
following sub-headline (verbatim, fetched 2026-05-09):

> "Die weiterhin ungeklärte Frage um mögliche Feuchtigkeitsschäden
> am Unesco-Erbe „The Family of Man" in Clerf hat jüngst Politik
> und Presse beschäftigt. Wäre im Worst Case das Unesco-Label des
> Dokumentenschatzes gefährdet?" (Lauer, *woxx*, 02.04.2026)

The article reports the immediate institutional context: a
mid-March 2026 session of the parliamentary cultural commission at
which CNA Director Gilles Zeimet and Culture Minister Eric Thill
(DP) had to respond to (verbatim, 2026-05-09)

> "der Vielzahl an Problemen, mit denen das CNA seit geraumer
> Zeit konfrontiert ist (woxx 1874 und 1876). Konkret ging es um
> gegen Zeimet gerichtete Sexismusvorwürfe, Managementprobleme,
> die abgebrochene Teutloff-Schau und eine Panne der Klimaanlage
> in der weltweit bekannten Fotoausstellung „The Family of Man"
> im Schloss Clerf im vergangenen Jahr." (Lauer, 02.04.2026)

The conservation incident is documented in technical detail
(verbatim, 2026-05-09):

> "Bei der Wartung der Klimaanlage im Juli 2025 sei aber bestätigt
> worden, dass einer der zwei Kompressoren der HVAC-Anlage defekt
> und nicht reparabel sei. Aus den Dokumenten, die auch der woxx
> vorliegen, wird ersichtlich, dass die Luftfeuchtigkeit an
> insgesamt acht Tagen über die Normalwerte von zwischen 45 und
> 55 Prozent auf zum Teil fast 70 Prozent gestiegen war.
> Präventive Maßnahmen seien laut Thill sofort ergriffen worden:
> Der noch funktionierende Kompressor habe unter voller Auslastung
> den Ausfall des anderen ausgeglichen. Am 25. Juli seien
> zusätzlich vier Entfeuchter installiert worden. Unter anderem
> hatte ein „Tageblatt"-Artikel darauf aufmerksam gemacht, dass an
> einem Tag, nämlich dem 21. Juli, nicht bloß einer, sondern beide
> Kompressoren kurzzeitig ausgefallen waren. Die Installierung
> zweier neuer Kompressoren wurde allerdings erst am 2. Dezember
> letzten Jahres abgeschlossen." (Lauer, 02.04.2026)

The parliamentary dimension is identified specifically (verbatim,
2026-05-09): the question came from the **CSV (centre-right) MP
Stéphanie Weydert**:

> "Die zeitweilige Fehlfunktion der Klimaanlage hat seit Ende
> Februar die Luxemburger Kulturpolitik beschäftigt: Die
> CSV-Abgeordnete Stéphanie Weydert hatte sich in einer
> parlamentarischen Anfrage bei Kulturminister Eric Thill (DP)
> nach dem Zustand der Fotos erkundigt und auf eine
> Klimaanlagenpanne im vergangenen Sommer verwiesen. Thill
> beschwichtigte: „Es gab keine dauerhafte Überschreitung der
> konservatorisch relevanten Grenzwerte, und die klimatischen
> Bedingungen blieben zu jedem Moment unter Kontrolle.""
> (Lauer, 02.04.2026)

The article also records Zeimet's admission that the CNA had no
in-house staff currently capable of conservation assessment of the
photographs (verbatim, 2026-05-09):

> "Zeimet gab zu, dass es derzeit keine*n CNA-Mitarbeiter*in
> gebe, der*die die Fotos auf Schäden untersuchen und sie
> gegebenenfalls restaurieren könnte." (Lauer, 02.04.2026)

The UNESCO Memory-of-the-World register implications are
addressed via Simone Beck, chair of the Luxembourgish UNESCO
commission (verbatim, 2026-05-09):

> "„Es gibt keinen nachgewiesenen Fall, in dem ein Dokument oder
> eine Dokumentensammlung wieder aus dem Register gestrichen
> wurde", erklärt die Expertin. Während das Unesco-Programm, das
> dem Schutz und dem Erhalt natürlicher sowie kultureller
> Welterbestätten dient, bei Verstößen gegen seine Konventionen
> explizit eine „Delisting"-Prozedur vorsehe, gebe es keine
> entsprechende Regelung hinsichtlich des Weltdokumentenerbes.
> Ohnehin würde es sich im Falle von „The Family of Man" nicht
> um mutwillige Zerstörung, sondern um eine technische Panne
> handeln, ergänzt sie." (Lauer, 02.04.2026)

The companion article — Joël Adami, "*The Family of Man*:
Demission und Diskussionen," *woxx* 24.04.2026 — reports on the
Wednesday 23 April 2026 cultural-commission session at which
Zeimet's resignation was announced. The opening paragraph (verbatim,
fetched 2026-05-09):

> "Gilles Zeimet ist als Direktor des Centre national de
> l'audiovisuel (CNA) zurückgetreten. Das gab Kulturminister
> Eric Thill (DP) am Mittwoch während der Sitzung der
> parlamentarischen Kulturkommission bekannt. Déi Lénk-Abgeordneter
> Marc Baum hatte die immer noch ungeklärte Frage zu möglichen
> Feuchtigkeitsschäden an den Exponaten der Ausstellung „The
> Family of Man" erneut auf die Tagesordnung gesetzt. Die
> Fotoschau des in Luxemburg geborenen Edward Steichen ist seit
> 2003 das einzige Luxemburger Dokumentenwerk im Unesco-Register
> „Memory of the World" – und wird es wohl auch bleiben (woxx
> 1881; Kulturpolitischer Problemfall)." (Adami, 24.04.2026)

Three observations belong on the reception thread.

First, the conservation crisis is a 2025 event that broke
parliamentarily in February–March 2026 and reached resolution at
the directorial level on 23 April 2026. The two parliamentary
interventions named in *woxx* are from different parties: CSV
(Weydert, in the Lauer article) and déi Lénk (Baum, in the Adami
article). The earlier draft of the project's source-file for the
Lauer article had conflated the two MPs; this round corrects the
attribution by direct fetch of both articles.

Second, the UNESCO Memory-of-the-World register inscription (2003)
remains intact. Beck's authoritative statement — that no document
or document-collection has ever been removed from the register —
is the most consequential institutional fact of the crisis: even
in a worst-case-physical-damage scenario, the inscription itself
is not procedurally at risk. The crisis is, on Beck's reading, a
conservation-and-governance crisis at CNA, not a UNESCO crisis.

Third, the Adami follow-up article reports Thill's apology in the
24 April commission session for a "Flüchtigkeitsfehler" during a
prior RTL interview (his statement that the photos had only been
exposed to elevated humidity for "wenige Minuten") and Baum's
counter-characterisation of Thill's behaviour as a "Vertrauensbruch"
on the basis of analysis of the temperature and humidity logs. The
crisis is therefore — at the close of this round — an open
political dispute over conservation transparency rather than a
settled question. The Adami article also records Thill's
commitment to an immediate "Monitoring" of the works, the
establishment of emergency procedures, and a real-time monitoring
system — and his own framing that "in einem Schloss könnten
niemals 'ideale Bedingungen wie in einem Museum' herrschen"
(verbatim, 2026-05-09). The latter remark belongs in the
reception thread as a direct ministerial reframing of the
2010–2013 Studio Berselli restoration's evidence-base: that
restoration's documented temperature-and-humidity tolerances are
the implicit standard against which Thill's "no museum-grade
ideal in a castle" is measured.

The Lauer and Adami pair is the most prominent 2020s
**Luxembourgish-press** critical reception entry on the
exhibition. It is not a critical-theory contribution; it is a
conservation-and-cultural-policy report. But the institutional
implications — the UNESCO Memory-of-the-World inscription's
continued procedural security, the CNA's gap in resident
conservation expertise, the post-Zeimet directorial transition,
and the public-trust deficit Baum identifies — are the substrate
of any 2020s reception synthesis that deals with the show's
present-day institutional life.

## 8. Where the 2020s reading sits relative to earlier decades

The 2020s extend the geographic-widening register of the 2010s
(Newbury on USIA-Africa, Takenaka on Tokyo) without producing a
2020s book-length successor to Sandeen 1995, Stimson 2006, or
Turner 2013. The most consequential shifts are not in the
chapter-level argument but in the institutional infrastructure
within which future scholarship will operate.

First, the FoMLEG project (§4) institutionalises in Luxembourg
the methodological apparatus the 2010s scholarship operated with
case by case. The 2018 *Family of Man Revisited* volume was a
single-volume scholarly intervention; FoMLEG is a multi-year,
FNR-funded research programme with its own deliverables (PhD
dissertation, deep-mapping website, graphic novel, and academic
publications). When FoMLEG's outputs appear, they will be the
first time since Sandeen 1995 that a substantial scholarly
re-reading of the exhibition's reception has been produced from
inside a custodial-institutional framework (CNA partnership). This
is the durable structural change of the decade.

Second, the AI-era critical reframing (§5) represents a
genuinely new development, not a re-iteration of earlier
critique. Lewandowski's "zombie humanism" is not a return to
Barthes 1957 or Sekula 1981 but a deployment of the Barthesian
*naturalisation* concept to a class of images Barthes did not
anticipate (post-photographic, generative-AI). The Turner 2010s
defence of the show's intentional design and audience invitation
is silent on this register; Turner's argument is about the 1955
installation, not about the post-photographic condition of FoM
images in 2023. Whether Lewandowski's intervention will produce
an interlocutor on the contrary side — a 2020s defence of
*A Generated Family of Man*'s humanist project, or an argument
that generative-AI images can re-anchor humanist photographic
politics rather than hollow them out — is open.

Third, the 2026 conservation-and-governance crisis (§6) returns
the reception thread to its mid-1990s starting point: the
question of what custody the CNA can and does provide for the
inscribed collection. The 2010–2013 Studio Berselli restoration
was a confident institutional moment; the April 2026
Zeimet resignation, the eight-day humidity excursion to nearly
70 percent, and the parliamentary "Vertrauensbruch" indictment
are its difficult reverse. The reception thread of the 1990s
(`research/reception-1990s-clervaux-installation.md`) treated
Clervaux installation as a redemptive ending; the 2026 crisis
suggests the redemption is itself a process, and one not yet
finished.

Fourth, on the long arc from Barthes 1957 forward, the 2020s
reading does not collapse the earlier divisions between
*October*-axis political critique, Sandeen / Stimson historical-
contextual reconstruction, and Turner Bauhaus-installation defence.
What it does is layer two new registers — the institutionalised
glocal-history programme (FoMLEG) and the post-photographic
critique (Lewandowski) — on top of the existing three. The five
registers are not collapsible into a single narrative. The 2020s
reception is best characterised as a decade that adds without
subtracting.
