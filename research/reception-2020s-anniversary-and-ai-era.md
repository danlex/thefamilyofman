# 2020s reception: the 70th anniversary, the FoMLEG turn, the AI-era humanism critique, and the Clervaux conservation crisis

> **Verification status of this essay.** The 2020s essay is the
> opposite case from the 2010s essay (`research/reception-2010s-turner-era.md`).
> Where the 2010s essay had a single freely-available scholarly anchor
> (Turner's 2012 *Public Culture* article) and most of its other 2010s
> texts were paywalled, the 2020s reception is partly composed of
> open-access digital-humanities scholarship and live-web journalism
> that *is* directly fetchable. Two of the four most important 2020s
> reception texts for this round — Lewandowski's "Zombie Humanism"
> (*Panorama* 2024) and the *woxx* Lauer/Adami pair (April 2026) — were
> retrieved as full text via `curl` on 2026-05-09 and are quoted
> verbatim with page numbers below. The 70th-anniversary popular-press
> features (Dench 2025, George 2025, West 2025) and the Newbury and
> Takenaka peer-reviewed scholarship are characterised at the level of
> their source-file content with explicit "not consulted in this
> round" markers; verbatim quotation from those texts is **not**
> asserted here.

This essay accompanies the 2020s bench of source entries
(`sources/2020s/`) and continues the decade-by-decade reception thread
established in `research/reception-1950s-us-press.md`,
`research/reception-1970s-critical-theory.md`,
`research/reception-1980s-critical-theory.md`,
`research/reception-1990s-clervaux-installation.md`,
`research/reception-2000s-stimson-era.md`, and
`research/reception-2010s-turner-era.md`. It is to be read alongside
`research/clervaux.md` (custodial history at the CNA, including the
2010–2013 restoration and now the 2025–2026 HVAC incident),
`research/world-tour.md` (FoMLEG's emerging archival map), and
`research/africa-gap.md` (the African and South African reception
thread that Newbury's 2023–2024 work substantially advances).

The 2020s are a four-strand decade. First, the 70th anniversary in
2025 generates the predictable popular-press cluster (Dench in
*Amateur Photographer*, George in *Digital Camera World*, West in
*Daily Art Magazine*, plus a Cercle Cité lecture covered by
*Chronicle.lu*) — celebratory in register, biographical and
photographer-centred in focus, and largely uncritical of the
humanist framing that has been under attack since 1957. Second, the
University of Luxembourg's C²DH launches the FoMLEG research
programme (announced October 2024) in collaboration with the CNA,
the institution holding the inscribed Clervaux collection — a
multi-year FNR-funded "glocal" reception study that explicitly
foregrounds the Global South question. Third, the AI-era humanism
critique opens, of which Helen Lewandowski's *Panorama* article
(Fall 2024) is the most fully developed instance: the Flickr
Foundation's AI-regenerated *Family of Man* (2023) becomes the lens
through which she diagnoses what she calls "zombie humanism." And
fourth, the decade ends — at least as of this round's cutoff
(May 2026) — with a conservation and governance crisis at Clervaux
itself: an HVAC compressor failure in summer 2025, a parliamentary
question on possible moisture damage, the resignation of CNA director
Gilles Zeimet on 24 April 2026, and a *woxx* investigation on
whether the UNESCO Memory-of-the-World listing is at risk.

These four strands do not collapse into a single narrative. They
co-exist; an honest 2020s reception essay has to keep them in tension.

## 1. The pre-2020 horizon, briefly

The reception thread carries the following positions into the 2020s:

- **Roland Barthes 1957** — the foundational critique of the show as
  myth-making about a transhistorical "human condition"
  [src-barthes-1957; not re-read this round].
- **Susan Sontag 1977 / Allan Sekula 1981 / Christopher Phillips 1982**
  — the *October*-axis political critique consolidating Barthes into
  a broader institutional argument about MoMA photography and US
  Cold War cultural diplomacy [not re-read this round].
- **Eric Sandeen 1995** — the historical-contextual book-length study
  treating the exhibition as a worked artefact requiring careful
  reconstruction [not re-read this round; characterised in
  `research/reception-1990s-clervaux-installation.md`].
- **Blake Stimson 2006** — the most-cited 2000s scholarly engagement,
  reading *The Family of Man* alongside *The Americans* and the
  Bechers as a study in how mass-photographic objects shape national
  self-understanding [not re-read this round].
- **Fred Turner 2012/2013** — the most-cited 2010s scholarly defence,
  reading the Rudolph hanging as a Bauhaus-inflected
  anti-fascist installation logic and the show as an attempt to
  produce "democratic personalities" [Turner 2012 was read in full
  in the prior round; book chapter 8 NOT borrowed via CDL this
  round].
- **Sarah E. James 2012, Eric Sandeen 2015 (Guatemala), Yasufumi
  Nakamori (Tokyo)** — the 2010s geographic widening into Berlin,
  Latin America, and Japan [not re-read this round; characterised in
  `research/reception-2010s-turner-era.md` §4].
- **Hurm, Reitz & Zamir (eds.) 2018, *The Family of Man Revisited:
  Photography in a Global Age*** — the I.B. Tauris essay collection
  whose stated framing is "challenging in particular the legacy of
  Roland Barthes's influential account of the exhibition" (CNA
  documentation page, fetched in the prior round 2026-05-09;
  characterised in `research/reception-2010s-turner-era.md` §5).

By 2020 the academic field carries (i) the *October*-axis political
critique, (ii) Sandeen's historical-contextual reconstruction,
(iii) Stimson and the Ribalta/MACBA reframing, (iv) Turner's
"democratic surround" defence, and (v) the geographic-widening
project of James / Sandeen-2015 / Nakamori / the *Family of Man
Revisited* contributors. The 2020s do not displace these. They add a
sixth and seventh axis (the African chapter consolidated by Newbury;
the AI-era humanism critique) and an eighth (the participatory /
deep-mapping digital-history approach of FoMLEG), and they re-anchor
the institutional voice through both a 70th-anniversary celebration
*and* a conservation crisis that breaks into national parliamentary
politics in Luxembourg.

## 2. The 70th anniversary, 2025: popular press and CNA-adjacent celebration

The 70th anniversary moment is observable through three strands of
2025 coverage, each at a different distance from the scholarly core.

The first strand is the UK photography-press cluster. Peter Dench in
*Amateur Photographer* (23 August 2025), under the headline "Is this
the greatest photo exhibition mankind ever created?", and Chris
George in *Digital Camera World* (1 July 2025), under "The world's
greatest-ever photo exhibition is 70 years old (and you can still go
and see it today!)," both pitch the anniversary as an invitation to
visit Clervaux [src-dench-2025-amateur-photographer-fom;
src-george-2025-digital-camera-world-fom; both source files note
that the article bodies were not directly fetched this round, and
their key characterisations therefore remain at the level of
search-snippet metadata]. Both titles uncritically reproduce the
1955 promotional framing — *Digital Camera World*'s "greatest-ever"
echoes the original catalog subtitle "The Greatest Photographic
Exhibition of All Time" — and both treat the Clervaux installation
as the canonical present-day form of the show. Neither title is in
*CREDIBILITY.md*'s Tier-2 list; both function in this essay as
Tier-3 reception data points only.

The second strand is the *Daily Art Magazine* feature: Mark I. West,
"Recognizing Dorothea Lange's Contributions to The Family of Man
Exhibition on Its 70th Anniversary" (13 October 2025)
[src-west-2025-dailyart-lange-fom; not directly fetched this round].
West's framing — that the 70th anniversary is "a fitting time to
recognize Lange's key role" — is itself a reception position: the
anniversary is read here as an opportunity for individual-photographer
recovery rather than for revisiting the *October*-axis critique or
the Global South question. The article is not by itself a
contribution to the critical-theory reception, but its existence is
data: in the major popular-art-history English-language web venue,
the 70th-anniversary occasion is taken up via Lange's biography
rather than via the ideological frame.

The third strand is the *Chronicle.lu* report on the Cercle Cité
lecture in Luxembourg (1 April 2025)
[src-chronicle-lu-2025-cercle-cite-steichen; the URL was directly
fetched in an earlier round 2026-04-25 and is verified=true]. The
article preserves three claims that anchor the Steichen-Luxembourg
narrative in popular memory: that Steichen "met Grand Duchess
Charlotte during a state visit to Washington in 1963, where he
introduced himself by stating: 'I am a Luxembourgish boy'"; that
Steichen's request "led to the US government's donation of *The
Family of Man* exhibition to Luxembourg in 1964"; and that "in 1966,
Edward Steichen was then honoured in Luxembourg and visited
Clervaux, where he reportedly stated that this was the ideal place
for the exhibition to reside" (chronicle.lu, fetched 2026-04-25).
The "ideal place" remark is reported as second-hand within the
*Chronicle.lu* article itself; it is one of the founding myths of
the Luxembourg installation and circulates uncritically through
popular Luxembourg coverage of the anniversary.

The popular-press 70th-anniversary cluster is, on aggregate,
celebratory and biographical. It does not engage Barthes, Sekula,
Phillips, or Newbury. It treats the show as a settled cultural
patrimony available for visit at Clervaux. Whether this is "the
2025 popular reception of *The Family of Man*" or "the 2025
popular reception of *the Clervaux installation*" is a question
the present essay flags but cannot fully resolve from these
sources alone.

## 3. The C²DH "70 Years" conference, 24 May 2025

A scholarly counterpart to the popular-press cluster runs through
the C²DH event on 24 May 2025: "70 Years of The Family of Man"
[src-c2dh-70-years-fom; the event page itself was not directly
fetched in this round]. The speakers list documented in the source
file — Shamoon Zamir (NYU/Abu Dhabi) on "Photography and
cosmopolitan citizenship: The politics of *The Family of Man*";
Claude Ewert (University of Luxembourg) on "The local reception of
the family of man: the FOMLEG project"; Paul Lesch on "Steichen, US
camera and *The Family of Man*"; Astrid Böger on "Robert Frank and
*The Family of Man*: A double take"; Gerd Hurm as keynote — is the
clearest single record of the international scholarly community
currently active on the exhibition's reception. The continuity with
the 2018 *Family of Man Revisited* volume is visible at the level of
personnel: Zamir was one of the three editors of that volume; Hurm
was another. The titles point at four live research strands —
cosmopolitan-citizenship theory (Zamir), Luxembourg-domestic
reception (Ewert / FoMLEG), the pre-1955 *US Camera* publishing
context (Lesch), and the Robert-Frank-versus-FoM comparison
(Böger). None of these has a confirmed published proceeding as of
this round; the conference functions in the bibliography as an
in-progress witness, not yet as a citable scholarly output.

## 4. The FoMLEG project: institutional digital-humanities reframing

The most consequential institutional development in 2020s *Family
of Man* scholarship is the FoMLEG (FoM Legacy) research project
launched by the C²DH at the University of Luxembourg in October
2024, in collaboration with the CNA. The project page
(https://www.uni.lu/c2dh-en/research-projects/fomleg/) was retrieved
via `curl` on 2026-05-09 in this round; the dated metadata embedded
in the page records "datePublished":"2024-04-10T07:46:40+00:00"
and "dateModified":"2025-10-16T06:20:44+00:00". The project's full
title, verbatim from the fetched page:

> "Tracing the legacy of Edward Steichen: A glocal approach to the
> international reception and national heritagisation of the
> 'Family of Man' exhibition (FoMLEG)"
> (C²DH project page, fetched 2026-05-09)

The project's stated scope, retrieved verbatim from the fetched page
(2026-05-09):

> "This multilayered and transdisciplinary research project aims to
> shed light on hitherto underexposed subjects of these collections
> and to direct its attention towards a twofold exploration: the
> international reception of the exhibition The Family of Man (FoM)
> during its world tour between 1955 and 1964 at selected locations
> and the heritagisation of Edward Steichen's oeuvre and legacy in
> the specific cultural and political contexts of Luxembourg and its
> evolution up until today."

Three details of this framing matter for the reception thread.

First, FoMLEG dates the world tour as "1955 and 1964" — the project
page's own bracket. The C²DH October 2024 announcement article
fetched in the prior round (`src-c2dh-fomleg-lasting-legacy`) gave
the figure of "37 countries," and the FoMLEG ArtHist.net call
(`src-arthist-fomleg-call-2025`) framed the dates as "1955–1963."
The three institutional FoMLEG documents thus carry three slightly
different end-dates for the tour (1963, 1964) — a small discrepancy
that `research/world-tour.md` should track.

Second, the project page makes explicit the "glocal" methodology, in
language that is the project's strongest single statement of its
position relative to the existing reception literature. The page
reads, verbatim (fetched 2026-05-09):

> "While building on the rich scholarship from the field of the
> history of photography, visual studies, and exhibition / museum
> studies, the project shifts the analytical focus to a more
> comparative perspective, aiming at historicizing processes of
> 'encoding' and 'decoding' of meaning at different spaces and
> places. In framing the FoM as a global media event, we aim to
> analyse the local performances of the exhibition as ritualised
> moments of thickened media communication, revealing specific
> strategies of territorial appropriation of globally circulating
> messages."

The "encoding / decoding" reference is to Stuart Hall's 1973
cultural-studies framework: FoMLEG's stated method is to read each
local installation as a distinct decoding event, not as a passive
recipient of a single curatorial message. This is a methodological
turn away from both the *October*-axis "the show transmitted the
message X" reading and the Turnerian "the show solicited
audience-as-co-author" reading. FoMLEG's wager is that what the
show *did* venue by venue can be reconstructed from local archival
records, surveys, press, installation photographs, and visitor
memory.

Third, the project page is explicit about the colonial / Global
South frame, verbatim (fetched 2026-05-09):

> "The project emphasises the need for a critical reflection on the
> colonial and post-colonial dimension of FoM by paying more
> attention to the question of how the exhibition has been received
> in the Global South."

This is FoMLEG's clearest single statement of where it sits
relative to the geographic-widening project opened in the 2010s by
James (Berlin), Sandeen-2015 (Guatemala), Nakamori (Tokyo), and
Newbury (Africa). FoMLEG inherits that turn and institutionalises
it: a multi-year FNR-funded research programme is now underway whose
explicit object is the Global South dimension that Anglophone
scholarship from Barthes through Sekula through Stimson largely did
not address.

The methodological description on the project page also commits the
team to specific outputs, verbatim (fetched 2026-05-09):

> "Through an interdisciplinary approach, combining visual studies,
> decolonial studies, intergenerational memory production,
> translation and reception studies, and transnational media
> history, the emphasis lies on the analysis of the historical
> reception as traced through official documents, surveys, press
> articles as well as installation images. By means of a
> participatory digital public history approach, first-hand accounts
> and memories of visitors will be collected and analysed [...]"

> "Next to academic publications and a PhD dissertation, the project
> will produce innovative forms and formats of transmedia
> storytelling, e.g. through the development of a deep mapping
> visualisation of the global circulation of FoM, combining a
> traceable itinerary of the exhibition around the world with
> virtual exhibits of those places of display studied as historical
> case studies in the project. In addition, a graphic novel will
> offer a research-based narrative about Steichen and the emergence
> of FoM as one of the most iconic photograph exhibitions of the
> 20th century."

The companion FoMLEG outputs documented in the source files —
Sánchez González's "Following the Family of Man World Tour"
(C²DH News, 3 March 2025; `src-c2dh-fomleg-world-tour`, directly
fetched in the previous round 2026-05-07) and the ArtHist.net "Call
for Memories" (`src-arthist-fomleg-call-2025`) — operationalise the
"participatory digital public history" approach. The Sánchez
González piece is also significant in its own right because it
quotes verbatim NARA RG 306 image captions that the project itself
was unable to obtain via direct fetch — including
"German playwright and poet Bertolt Brecht crosses from East Berlin
to visit 'The Family of Man' exhibition at the Hochschule für
Bildende Künste in West Berlin, Germany. 1955. Records of the U.S.
Information Agency. Courtesy National Archives, photo no. 306-FM-3"
(C²DH news article, fetched 2026-05-07; carried in
`src-c2dh-fomleg-world-tour` Key excerpts). FoMLEG, in other words,
is also a Tier-3 institutional witness *to* Tier-1 primary
(USIA/NARA) records that this project's tooling has not been able
to access directly.

The institutional shape of FoMLEG matters for the broader
reception thread. The C²DH and the CNA — the holders of the
UNESCO-inscribed collection at Clervaux — are now formally
collaborating on a research programme whose explicit aim is to
contextualise that collection comparatively and post-colonially.
This is a different institutional posture from the 2010s, when the
CNA's institutional voice (the `src-cna-collections-eng-family-of-man`
collections page, fetched in the prior round) was largely focused
on conservation history and the Studio Berselli restoration team.
FoMLEG re-positions Luxembourg as a node in a critical-research
network, not only as a custodial site.

What FoMLEG has *not yet* produced, as of this round's cutoff
(May 2026), is peer-reviewed published outputs of its own. The PhD
dissertation (Ewert), the deep-mapping visualisation, the graphic
novel, and the academic publications are all on the project's
horizon, not yet in print. The 2020s reception essay therefore
treats FoMLEG as the institutional and methodological frame of the
decade, while retaining the labelling "outputs not yet consulted in
this round" for the substance of its findings.

## 5. The African chapter consolidates: Newbury 2023 and 2024

Where the 2010s essay opened the African chapter at the level of the
adjacent Enwezor / Bester *Rise and Fall of Apartheid* catalog
(2013), the 2020s decade is when Darren Newbury's
USIA-Africa-photography research, ongoing since the late 2010s,
becomes the dominant scholarly anchor for the Johannesburg /
South African / continental-Africa reception of *The Family of
Man*. Three texts mark the consolidation:

- Newbury 2023, "Ernest Cole's *House of Bondage*, the United States
  Information Agency, and the Cultural Politics of Race in the Cold
  War," in Phu, Duganne and Noble (eds.), *Cold War Camera*
  (Duke UP, 2023), pp. 33–65 [src-newbury-2023-cole-cold-war-camera;
  body text NOT consulted in this round].
- Newbury 2024 (a), "An African Looks at America: Picturing Racial
  Integration for Africa, 1956–68," *History of Photography* 47,
  no. 3 (2024): 269–297 [src-newbury-2024-african-looks-at-america;
  body text NOT consulted in this round; Tandfonline paywalled].
- Newbury 2024 (b), *Cold War Photographic Diplomacy: The US
  Information Agency and Africa* (Penn State UP, 2024), 300 pp.,
  ISBN 978-0-271-09567-7 [src-newbury-2024-cold-war-photographic-diplomacy;
  body text NOT consulted in this round; the PSU Press catalog page
  WAS retrieved in this round 2026-05-09 and is the source for the
  publisher's description and chapter list].

The PSU Press page for Newbury 2024 (b), fetched 2026-05-09,
contains the publisher's description verbatim:

> "The emergence of newly independent African nations onto the world
> stage in the mid-twentieth century precipitated a contest for
> influence among Cold War superpowers, leading the United States to
> mount an international campaign of photographic diplomacy
> underpinned by a faith in the medium's capacity to cross cultural
> boundaries. However, the increasing global visibility of racial
> injustice undermined US claims that the nation had transcended
> colonial racism."

> "Drawing on extensive research in the archives of the United
> States Information Agency (USIA) and concentrating on the period
> from the mid-1950s through to the late 1960s, Darren Newbury
> traces the role of photography in the United States' appeal to
> Africa."

The PSU Press page also lists the chapter titles verbatim
(fetched 2026-05-09):

> "1. Photography, Race, and the Cold War Imagination"
> "2. Photography, Public Diplomacy, and the Africa Program at the
> United States Information Agency"
> "3. 'Toward a Better World': Africa, the United Nations, and the
> Photographic Diplomacy of Decolonization"
> "4. 'A Pleasant Mixture of Negro and White': Photographing Civil
> Rights as Democracy in Action"
> "5. 'Africans at the Wax Museum': Photography and International
> Friendship"
> "6. 'Don't Touch Those Windows': United States Information Service
> Exhibits in Africa"

A factual correction to the existing source-file content is
warranted on this point. The earlier source file
(`src-newbury-2024-cold-war-photographic-diplomacy`) carried a
search-snippet description that named *The Family of Man* in the
publisher blurb. The verbatim PSU Press description retrieved in
this round does **not** name Steichen or *The Family of Man*; it
foregrounds racial-integration photographic diplomacy and African
decolonisation. The chapter titles likewise do not name *The Family
of Man*. This does not mean Newbury 2024 (b) does not discuss the
exhibition — it almost certainly does, given the chronological
window and the USIA-Africa focus, and the related Newbury 2023
chapter on Cole and the USIA Johannesburg office is explicit about
the *Family of Man* connection in search-snippet abstracts that
should be re-verified by direct fetch — but the **publisher's
framing of the book** treats *Family of Man* not as the book's
object but as the more famous flagship that the book deliberately
moves past in order to reconstruct the rest of the USIA's African
photographic apparatus.

The single 2020s scholarly framing of the relationship between
Newbury's project and *Family of Man* that is verifiable from the
PSU Press page alone is implicit: Newbury's book is a contribution
to "the history of photography, American studies, and Africana
studies" (publisher description, fetched 2026-05-09) drawn from
"extensive research in the archives of the United States
Information Agency (USIA)" — meaning Newbury has conducted the
NARA RG 306 archival work that this project's own tooling has not
been able to complete (see `src-nara-rg306-africa-access-barrier`
and `research/world-tour.md` §9). Newbury's footnotes are likely
to cite specific RG 306 finding-aid numbers relevant to the African
tour stops. A future pass that retrieves the book by interlibrary
loan should extract those references and cross-reference them
against the Sánchez González / FoMLEG NARA visit findings.

The Newbury 2023 *Cold War Camera* chapter is, on its source-file
content (`src-newbury-2023-cole-cold-war-camera` Key excerpts), the
most directly *Family-of-Man*-engaged of the three Newbury texts.
The Duke UP abstract — captured in the source file via search
results, not directly fetched — describes the chapter as
examining "Cole's interactions with the United States Information
Agency (USIA), an arm of US Cold War public diplomacy better known
for its sponsorship of Edward Steichen's Family of Man exhibition,
via their offices in Johannesburg in the period immediately prior
to his exile." The chapter's stated argument is that Ernest Cole's
*House of Bondage* (1967) functions as a counter-archive to the
USIA's humanist-universalist visual production, of which *Family
of Man* was the flagship. The "antidote" formulation circulating in
search-result prose around the chapter is flagged as "verified:
false" in the source file and should not be cited as a Newbury
quotation until the chapter text is directly accessed.

The 2020s African-chapter consolidation does not displace the
earlier reception strands; it adds the most substantial scholarly
work yet on the USIA's continental-Africa photographic activity
within which the August 1958 Johannesburg showing of *Family of
Man* is now situatable. Where the 2010s essay had Enwezor / Bester
2013 as the adjacent anchor for the Johannesburg context, the
2020s essay has Newbury's Cole chapter (2023), the Penn State book
(2024), and the *History of Photography* article (2024) as a
three-part scholarly bench — once the body texts of those three
publications are obtained.

## 6. Takenaka 2022 and the atomic-bomb reading of the Tokyo stop

Yumi Kim Takenaka's chapter "Shadows of the Atomic Bombings in *The
Family of Man*: The American Photographic Exhibition Tour of Japan
in the Post-Occupation Period," in *Capture Japan* (Bloomsbury
Academic, 2022), is the early-2020s anchor for the Japanese
reception of the show [src-takenaka-2022-atomic-bombings; body text
NOT consulted in this round]. The chapter is in the same scholarly
neighbourhood as Nakamori's Graham-Foundation Tokyo / Kenzo Tange
project from the 2010s, but it foregrounds a different question:
not the exhibition design (Tange) but the curatorial controversy
that emerged shortly after the Tokyo opening — the removal of
Yosuke Yamahata's atomic-bomb photographs of Nagasaki, which had
been added specifically for the Japanese installation, and the
press protest that followed.

The source file records two characterisations from search-result
snippets retrieved 2026-04-30, neither of which has been verified
against the chapter's body text in any session. The first
characterisation: "The exhibition attracted one million visitors in
Japan and the immediate removal of some photographs of the atomic
bombing on Nagasaki, which were specially added to the
installation, caused controversy. This characterized the reception
of *The Family of Man* in Japan as being in cultural tensions
between the aspirations for nuclear energy and the fears of nuclear
disaster in the 1950s." The second: a 29 April 1956 issue of *Asahi
Graph* "posted a picture of the wall with the headline 'Your
Highness the Emperor and Prince Yoshi, Please Look at These' so
that readers could see what was removed."

If these characterisations are accurate to Takenaka's argument —
and the body text is needed to confirm — then the Tokyo case
substantially complicates Steichen's stated 1955 framing of the
hydrogen-bomb transparency at MoMA as "the moral choice" the
exhibition was placing on the visitor. The Tokyo installation
*added* atomic-bomb photographs that were *not* in the New York
version; those additions were then *removed*; and the *Asahi
Graph* protest treated the removal as a piece of cultural-political
news worth a wall-of-photographs feature. This is exactly the
kind of venue-specific reception data that Sandeen-2015 demonstrated
for Guatemala and that FoMLEG's "glocal" methodology proposes to
generalise. The Japan case has, according to the source file, the
fullest scholarly recovery of any non-US tour stop in the
2020s peer-reviewed literature — but the chapter remains unread
in this project, and verbatim quotation from it is not asserted
here.

## 7. The AI-era humanism critique: Lewandowski 2024, "Zombie Humanism"

The single most important 2020s text on *The Family of Man* for
which body-text access is verifiable in this project is Helen
Lewandowski, "Zombie Humanism, Generative AI Images, and
Photography. Review of *A Generated Family of Man* By the Flickr
Foundation," *Panorama: Journal of the Association of Historians
of American Art* 10, no. 2 (Fall 2024). DOI:
https://doi.org/10.24926/24716839.19237. The PDF is freely hosted
on the journal's site and was retrieved via `curl` and read in this
round on 2026-05-09 (pages 1–9, the article's full extent).

Lewandowski's article is a review of the Flickr Foundation's
*A Generated Family of Man* (volume 3 of the foundation's "A Flickr
of Humanity" New Curators Programme, published October 2023; archived
at archive.org/details/a-generated-family-of-man), a project in
which Goldsmiths (University of London) design students used
Microsoft Bing's Image Creator (built on OpenAI's DALL-E) to attempt
to recreate "around half of the photographs that appeared in *The
Family of Man* publication" (Lewandowski 2024, p. 2). The article
reads the AI re-generation as the lens through which to examine
*Family of Man*'s long critical afterlife.

The article opens with an explicit reception-history framing of
*Family of Man* that names the canonical critics
(Lewandowski 2024, p. 1):

> "Critique of *The Family of Man* and its themes seems to return
> like clockwork every few years — each endeavoring to offer new
> insight on the subject. First shown from January 24 to May 8,
> 1955, at the Museum of Modern Art (MoMA) in New York, the
> exhibition curated by Edward Steichen intended to reveal the
> commonalities between peoples across the world through (mostly
> documentary and photojournalistic) photography. Part of *The
> Family of Man*'s enduring influence rests, however, not only on
> its public impact as a successful touring exhibition (on permanent
> display in Luxembourg since 1994) but also as a heuristic device
> for critiquing photography as a whole. In 1956, Roland Barthes
> appraised this type of humanist photography as a conservative
> abstraction that naturalized insidious elements of this 'family';
> in 1981, Allan Sekula dissected 'the oft-repeated claim that
> photography constitutes a 'universal language''; in 2004, Abigail
> Solomon-Godeau viewed its permanent display as 'a kind of Benetton
> ad' with advertising co-opting this 'representational work'; in
> the 2010s, Ariella Azoulay provided a critical rehabilitation of
> the exhibition, arguing that it should be understood by its
> multiplicity and global citizenry. The list goes on."
> (Lewandowski 2024, p. 1)

Several particulars of this list belong on the reception thread.
First, Lewandowski's note 1 dates the show "1955" and the
permanent display at Clervaux to "1994" (the ground-floor
inauguration; the upper floors followed later, see
`research/clervaux.md`); her note 1 also lists "37 countries,"
mirroring the C²DH figure (`src-c2dh-fomleg-lasting-legacy`) and
the unanchored secondary-literature consensus
(`research/world-tour.md` §3). Second, Lewandowski dates Barthes's
critique to **1956**, not 1957 — this is the date of the original
French publication of the underlying *Le Petit Parisien* essay
treatment, but the canonical *Mythologies* citation is 1957; the
project's own files (e.g.
`research/reception-1980s-critical-theory.md`) use 1957. The
discrepancy is a known one in the secondary literature and does
not affect the substance of Barthes's argument; it is flagged here
only for cross-referencing. Third, Lewandowski's most important
addition to the canonical list is Ariella Azoulay's 2010s
"critical rehabilitation" — read in her note 2 as Azoulay's
"'The Family of Man': A Visual Universal Declaration of Human
Rights," in *The Human Snapshot* (Berlin: LUMA Foundation; Center
for Curatorial Studies, Bard College; Sternberg, 2013), and
"'Photography Is Not Served': 'The Family of Man' and The Human
Condition," in *Documentary Across Disciplines*, ed. Erika Balsom
and Hila Peleg (London: MIT Press, 2016) (Lewandowski 2024, p. 8,
note 2). Azoulay is named here for the first time on this project's
reception thread; previous decade essays have not yet integrated
her work, and a future pass should add her as a 2010s-into-2020s
strand alongside Turner.

Lewandowski's central diagnostic is the term "zombie humanism."
The most direct statement of what she means by it is on p. 6
(verbatim, fetched 2026-05-09):

> "Twenty-first-century humanism has focused on neoliberal
> individualism, subjectivity, and first-person narratives,
> emphasizing the voices of both subject and author. While this
> humanist resurgence may be criticized as merely gestural, the
> subsequent images produced by programs like DALL-E and Midjourney
> artificially reanimate this digitally overrepresented 2010s
> culture, a mixture of scraped photographs from search engines,
> social-media platforms, and stock photography. This is what I
> would term 'zombie humanism,' incidentally producing the same
> commercially motivated 'everyday' tropes but presenting them
> untethered from historical, political, or photographic contexts
> (a nineteenth-century Alice Liddell revivified through Tumblr; a
> 1940s dead soldier transmorphed into a 2010s influencer)."
> (Lewandowski 2024, p. 6)

The "1940s dead soldier transmorphed into a 2010s influencer"
example is concrete: Raphael Platnick's 1944 photograph of a dead
US soldier face down at the battle of Eniwetok, in *Family of Man*'s
catalog at pp. 180–81, is regenerated by AI via human and AI
prompts respectively into "a dynamic head-on perspective of a
helmet" (human prompt) and "a lifestyle magazine-type image of a
blonde woman against a field, captioned: 'Taking a moment to
connect with nature's embrace #FieldOfDreams #GroundedInTheWilderness
#SerenityInTheGrass'" (AI prompt) (Lewandowski 2024, pp. 4–5,
fetched 2026-05-09). The Warsaw Ghetto image (catalog pp. 166–67)
is similarly displaced by AI generation into a stripped, identical-
men image with no children (Lewandowski 2024, pp. 4–5).

Lewandowski's explicit positioning of "zombie humanism" against
Stimson and Azoulay is on p. 7, verbatim:

> "It may be tempting to view generative AI images through the lens
> of posthumanism, which, according to Rosi Braidotti, could move
> past the 'lethal binaries' of humanism/antihumanism toward 'an
> eco-philosophy of multiple belongings.' Rather than a Braidottian
> understanding of posthumanism, liberated from the conservative
> and anthropocentric humanism associated with postwar humanist
> photography, there is instead a risk of further entrenching
> harmful humanist biases, reifying what people want to see or
> believe. This is neither a return to the 'old humanist
> institutions that created the conditions for greater equality,'
> which Stimson reminisced about, nor Azoulay's reformed humanism,
> which rejects postwar humanism's implicit white colonial man in
> favor of the heroic figure of the citizen. Instead, generative
> AI's zombie humanism is, at best, as per Barthes's 1956 text, a
> further *naturalization* of ingrained human biases. At worst,
> however, it inhabits the visual signifiers of humanism while
> actively discrediting the photojournalistic corpus that documents
> our shared human history."
> (Lewandowski 2024, p. 7)

The essay's two-step verdict — at best, a Barthesian
naturalisation of bias; at worst, the visual cannibalisation of
the photojournalistic corpus — is the cleanest 2020s expression of
why the *Family of Man* critical thread remains live in the
generative-AI moment. Lewandowski's argument is not that
*Family of Man* "caused" generative-AI humanism; it is that the
Flickr Foundation's regeneration project performs, in 2023 image
production, exactly the kind of universalist framing that Barthes
diagnosed in 1957 (or 1956), Sekula in 1981, and
Solomon-Godeau in 2004 — and that the AI tooling reproduces the
*aesthetic signs* of that humanism while severing them from the
historical specificity that gave the original photographs their
documentary force.

Two further details from the article are worth recording. The first
is Lewandowski's framing of *Family of Man* itself as a "beloved
punching bag of photographic critique" (Lewandowski 2024, p. 1),
which is the cleanest single phrase in 2020s scholarship for the
status of the show in the 2020s critical canon: a settled object
of critique, but one that scholars keep returning to because new
events — generative AI in this case — keep re-activating its
unresolved questions. The second is Lewandowski's note that
"Famously, the only color photograph in the original *Family of
Man* exhibition (which does not appear in the catalogue) was a
massive backlit transparency of an H-bomb test blast, displayed in
the final room. Blake Stimson describes this placement as a 'moral
choice' for the viewer, in which 'nuclear holocaust was set up as
the alternative to living the collective fantasy of a family of
man'" (Lewandowski 2024, pp. 2–3, citing Stimson, *The Pivot of
the World*, p. 101). The Stimson page-101 anchor — fetched here
through Lewandowski rather than directly from Stimson's text —
should be treated as second-hand and re-verified against
*Pivot of the World* in a future round (see
`research/reception-2000s-stimson-era.md` §3, where Stimson 2006's
body text was not consulted).

The Lewandowski article is as close as the 2020s decade has come,
within this project's verifiable bibliography, to a direct
*October*-axis-tradition critique of the show's contemporary
afterlife. Note carefully, however, that "*October*-axis tradition"
here refers to the lineage of the political / institutional critique
of mass-photographic humanism, not to the journal *October* itself:
Lewandowski's article appears in *Panorama*, an open-access
DOAJ-indexed AHAA publication, and *Panorama*'s peer-review status
at the article level was a contested issue in this project's
internal credibility-judge process (see
`src-lewandowski-2024-zombie-humanism` Tier justification, where
the entry was demoted from Tier 2 to Tier 3 on PR #127 because the
article appears in the *Panorama* "Digital Dialogues" section,
which is editor-reviewed rather than peer-reviewed at the article
level). The article is treated here as a Tier-3 named-scholar
publication with full verbatim text retrieved in-session.

## 8. The Clervaux conservation crisis, summer 2025 – April 2026

The 2020s decade closes — at this round's cutoff, May 2026 — with
a conservation incident at Clervaux that escalates into a
cultural-policy crisis covered most fully by the Luxembourgish
weekly *woxx*. Two articles by different authors mark the public
phase of the crisis; both were retrieved verbatim via `curl` in
this round (2026-05-09).

The first is Chris Lauer, "»The Family of Man«: Kulturpolitischer
Problemfall," *woxx*, 2 April 2026
[src-woxx-2026-fom-kulturpolitischer-problemfall, retrieved
verbatim 2026-05-09]. The article opens by stating, verbatim
(fetched 2026-05-09):

> "Ob die Exponate der Fotoausstellung „The Family of Man" im
> Schloss Clerf Schäden durch eine erhöhte Luftfeuchtigkeit
> davongetragen haben, ist noch unklar. In einer Sitzung der
> parlamentarischen Kulturkommission Mitte März mussten Gilles
> Zeimet, umstrittener Direktor des Centre national de l'audiovisuel
> (CNA), und Kulturminister Eric Thill (DP) Stellung beziehen zu der
> Vielzahl an Problemen, mit denen das CNA seit geraumer Zeit
> konfrontiert ist (woxx 1874 und 1876). Konkret ging es um gegen
> Zeimet gerichtete Sexismusvorwürfe, Managementprobleme, die
> abgebrochene Teutloff-Schau und eine Panne der Klimaanlage in der
> weltweit bekannten Fotoausstellung „The Family of Man" im Schloss
> Clerf im vergangenen Jahr."

In English: whether the photographs at Clervaux Castle have been
damaged by elevated humidity is still unclear; the parliamentary
culture commission in mid-March 2026 required Zeimet (CNA director)
and Culture Minister Eric Thill (DP) to take a position on
sexism allegations, management problems, the cancelled Teutloff
exhibition, and an HVAC failure during the previous summer (2025).
The Lauer article confirms verbatim that there is currently no CNA
employee qualified to inspect or restore the photographs:

> "Zeimet gab zu, dass es derzeit keine*n CNA-Mitarbeiter*in gebe,
> der*die die Fotos auf Schäden untersuchen und sie gegebenenfalls
> restaurieren könnte."
> (Lauer, *woxx*, 2 April 2026, fetched 2026-05-09)

The article also records, verbatim, a CNA written response to woxx
on 23 March 2026:

> "„Die Betreuung der Sammlung basiert auf einer Kombination von
> ständigem internem Management und, wenn nötig, spezialisierter
> externer Expertise. Eine detaillierte Monitoring-Kampagne ist im
> Rahmen der nächsten jährlichen Schließung vorgesehen.""
> (Lauer, *woxx*, 2 April 2026, fetched 2026-05-09, quoting CNA)

The UNESCO question is treated explicitly in the article through an
interview with Simone Beck, chair of the Luxembourg UNESCO
commission. Beck's reassurance, verbatim:

> "„Es gibt keinen nachgewiesenen Fall, in dem ein Dokument oder
> eine Dokumentensammlung wieder aus dem Register gestrichen
> wurde", erklärt die Expertin. Während das Unesco-Programm, das
> dem Schutz und dem Erhalt natürlicher sowie kultureller
> Welterbestätten dient, bei Verstößen gegen seine Konventionen
> explizit eine „Delisting"-Prozedur vorsehe, gebe es keine
> entsprechende Regelung hinsichtlich des Weltdokumentenerbes.
> Ohnehin würde es sich im Falle von „The Family of Man" nicht um
> mutwillige Zerstörung, sondern um eine technische Panne handeln,
> ergänzt sie."
> (Lauer, *woxx*, 2 April 2026, fetched 2026-05-09)

The substantive UNESCO point Beck makes is that Memory of the World
inscriptions, unlike World Heritage Sites, do not have a formal
delisting procedure — even in cases of intentional destruction
(Beck's phrasing: "selbst im Falle einer absichtlichen Vernichtung
könne sie nicht eingreifen"). The Memory-of-the-World status of
*The Family of Man* — inscribed 2003, the only Luxembourgish
documentary work in the register — is therefore not directly
threatened by the Clervaux HVAC incident, on Beck's institutional
reading. Whether the photographs themselves have suffered damage
remains, as of the article's date, an open question.

The article also fixes the political framing: the parliamentary
inquiry was initiated by CSV deputy Stéphanie Weydert
("Die CSV-Abgeordnete Stéphanie Weydert hatte sich in einer
parlamentarischen Anfrage bei Kulturminister Eric Thill (DP) nach
dem Zustand der Fotos erkundigt und auf eine Klimaanlagenpanne im
vergangenen Sommer verwiesen"); the minister's defensive position
is also quoted verbatim ("„Es gab keine dauerhafte Überschreitung
der konservatorisch relevanten Grenzwerte, und die klimatischen
Bedingungen blieben zu jedem Moment unter Kontrolle.""). Both
quotations are from Lauer, *woxx*, 2 April 2026 (fetched
2026-05-09).

The second article is Joël Adami, "»The Family of Man«: Demission
und Diskussionen," *woxx*, 24 April 2026 [retrieved verbatim
2026-05-09]. The article opens with the resignation announcement:

> "Gilles Zeimet ist als Direktor des Centre national de
> l'audiovisuel (CNA) zurückgetreten. Das gab Kulturminister Eric
> Thill (DP) am Mittwoch während der Sitzung der parlamentarischen

The Clervaux conservation crisis is, on the face of the *woxx*
record, the first time in the 2020s that *The Family of Man*'s
custodial situation at the Luxembourg national institution becomes

project's reception thread has had to engage Luxembourg

minister apologises for a misstatement; the UNESCO commission
chair clarifies the limits of the Memory-of-the-World programme.
None of this changes the photographs' status as inscribed
documentary heritage, but it changes the institutional voice
through which the heritage is mediated.

## 10. Where the 2020s reading sits relative to earlier decades

The 2020s reading does not produce a single new theoretical frame
of the Turner / Stimson kind. It is, instead, characterised by four
parallel movements — three scholarly, one institutional / political
— that re-anchor the *Family of Man* problem in domains that the
earlier decade essays did not need to address.

First, the AI-era critique (Lewandowski 2024) re-actualises the
Barthes / Sekula / Solomon-Godeau lineage in the specific moment
when the documentary force of photography is itself under
existential pressure from generative-image production. Lewandowski
explicitly treats *Family of Man* as the "beloved punching bag of
photographic critique" (p. 1) — a phrase that captures the show's
peculiar 2020s status better than any earlier formula. The show is
no longer the live polemical object it was in the 1957 / 1981 /
1982 critical moment, nor is it the live recovery object it
briefly was in Turner 2012. It is the canonical reference point
through which a new diagnostic — "zombie humanism" — is articulated
about an object (AI image generation) that did not exist when
the canonical critiques were written. Lewandowski's article is the
first 2020s text in this project's verifiable bibliography to make
this re-actualisation explicit.

Second, the African-chapter consolidation (Newbury 2023, 2024a,
2024b) closes a gap that had been visible in the project's research
since at least the `africa-gap.md` round (2026-04). The 2020s now
contains the most archivally substantial scholarly engagement with
the USIA's continental-Africa photographic apparatus, and that
engagement is by an author — Newbury — who has done the NARA RG 306
work that this project itself has been blocked from. The 2020s
African chapter does not yet supply Lewandowski-style verbatim
content in this round (the body texts are paywalled or in
controlled-access form), but its institutional shape — Penn State
UP monograph, *History of Photography* article, Duke UP edited-
volume chapter — is fully verifiable.

Third, the FoMLEG turn re-positions Luxembourg as an institutional
node not only of custodial heritage but of comparative
post-colonial reception research. The University of Luxembourg's
C²DH explicitly commits, in the project page text retrieved in this
round (2026-05-09), to "a critical reflection on the colonial and
post-colonial dimension of FoM by paying more attention to the
question of how the exhibition has been received in the Global
South." That commitment — verbatim from the project's own page —
is the clearest 2020s institutional statement about the inadequacy
of the prior (largely Anglophone, largely Northern-Atlantic)
reception literature on this exact dimension.

Fourth, the Clervaux conservation crisis (woxx, April 2026) inserts
Luxembourg national parliamentary politics into the reception
thread for the first time. The CNA's institutional voice — which
the 1990s, 2000s, and 2010s essays could rely on as a stable
custodial backstop — is now, on the *woxx* record, a contested
voice. The director resigns; the minister apologises; the
opposition deputies treat the moisture data as evidence of
conservation failure; the UNESCO commission chair clarifies that
delisting is procedurally not on the table. The reception thread
must now register that the institution holding the inscribed
collection is, at the close of this decade, in active institutional
turbulence.

The four movements do not merge. A reader of the 2020s reception
literature has to keep them visible at once. The 70th-anniversary
popular press (Dench, George, West, Cercle Cité / *Chronicle.lu*)
is celebratory and biographical; the AI-era humanism critique
(Lewandowski) is severe and Barthesian; the African-chapter and
FoMLEG scholarship is archival and post-colonial; the Clervaux
crisis is journalistic and political. The exhibition has, by 2026,
acquired four simultaneous afterlives — popular, AI-critical,
archivally-recuperative, and institutionally-precarious — that the
earlier decade essays could not have anticipated.
