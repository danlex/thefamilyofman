# 2010s reception: the Turner era, the *Democratic Surround*, and the global-reception turn

> **A note on the framing "Turner era".** The phrase is used here as a
> convenience handle for the decade in which Fred Turner's *The Democratic
> Surround* (University of Chicago Press, 2013) sits as the most-cited
> single 2010s monograph engaging *The Family of Man*. Turner's *Public
> Culture* article of 2012 — which contains the same argument in
> compressed form and is freely available as a PDF from the author's
> Stanford page — was retrieved in full and read directly in this round
> (see §3). The chapter "The Museum of Modern Art Makes the World a
> Family" in the book itself was **not** borrowed via Internet Archive
> Controlled Digital Lending in this round; book-level claims are
> therefore framed at the level of metadata and chapter-title verification
> only. The article's argument and the book's argument are not assumed to
> be identical here, even where the secondary literature treats them as
> interchangeable.

This essay accompanies the 2010s bench of source entries
(`sources/2010s/`) and continues the decade-by-decade reception thread
established in `research/reception-1950s-us-press.md`,
`research/reception-1970s-critical-theory.md`,
`research/reception-1980s-critical-theory.md`,
`research/reception-1990s-clervaux-installation.md`, and
`research/reception-2000s-stimson-era.md`. It is to be read alongside
`research/clervaux.md` (custodial history at the CNA, including the
2010–2013 restoration), `research/world-tour.md` (the tour record that
2010s scholarship begins to redraw venue by venue), and `research/africa-gap.md`
(South African and broader African reception, partially worked through
in the 2010s by Newbury and others).

The 2010s are the decade in which three movements happen at once.
First, the most sustained scholarly defence of *The Family of Man*
since Sandeen 1995 appears, primarily in the work of Fred Turner.
Second, the geographic frame of the show's reception widens
substantially — Sarah E. James opens the Berlin / divided-Germany
chapter, Eric Sandeen opens the Latin American chapter, Yasufumi
Nakamori opens the Tokyo / Kenzo Tange chapter, and Newbury's
Africa-focused work (entered in the 2020s sources bench but
substantially worked out in the 2010s) opens the African chapter.
Third, the Clervaux installation closes for restoration in
September 2010 and reopens in July 2013 — the institutional event of
the decade for the Luxembourg afterlife. The three movements are
related but not the same gesture.

## 1. The pre-2010 horizon, briefly

The reception thread carries the following positions into the 2010s:

- **Roland Barthes 1957** — the foundational critique of the show as
  myth-making about a transhistorical "human condition"
  [src-barthes-1957; not re-read this round].
- **Susan Sontag 1977 / Allan Sekula 1981 / Christopher Phillips 1982**
  — the *October*-axis political critique consolidating Barthes into a
  broader institutional argument about MoMA photography and US Cold War
  cultural diplomacy [src-sontag-1977, src-sekula-1981, src-phillips-1982-judgment-seat;
  not re-read this round].
- **Eric Sandeen 1995** — the historical-contextual book-length study
  treating the exhibition as a worked artefact requiring careful
  reconstruction, reviewed in *AHR* and *Journal of American Studies*
  [src-sandeen-1995; characterized at the level of overall argument in
  `research/reception-1990s-clervaux-installation.md`].
- **Blake Stimson 2006** — the most-cited 2000s scholarly engagement,
  reading *The Family of Man* alongside *The Americans* and the Bechers
  as a study in how mass-photographic objects shape national
  self-understanding [src-stimson-2006; body text unread in this
  project so far, see `research/reception-2000s-stimson-era.md` §3].
- **Jorge Ribalta (ed.) 2008** — *Public Photographic Spaces*, the
  MACBA volume reframing *Family of Man* as the closing term in a
  longer 1928–55 sequence of politically-instrumentalised mass-photo
  exhibitions [src-ribalta-2008-public-photographic-spaces; body text
  unread].

By 2010 the academic field carries three live readings: the
Barthes/Sekula/Phillips political critique, the Sandeen
historical-contextual reconstruction, and the Stimson/Ribalta
2000s reappraisal. The 2010s do not collapse these — they add a
fourth, primarily through Turner.

## 2. Turner 2012: what the *Public Culture* article says, fetched verbatim

The single most important 2010s text on *The Family of Man* — at
least at the level of body-text access verifiable in this project — is
Fred Turner, "*The Family of Man* and the Politics of Attention in
Cold War America," *Public Culture* 24, no. 1 (2012): 55–84. The PDF
is freely hosted on Turner's Stanford faculty page and was retrieved
via `curl` and read in full in this round
[src-turner-2012-politics-attention; PDF fetched 2026-05-09 at
fredturner.stanford.edu/.../turner-family-of-man-pc-24.1.pdf].

Turner's project is announced in the second paragraph and is worth
quoting at length, because it is the cleanest statement of his
position relative to the 1957 / 1981 / 1982 critical tradition:

> "Without for a moment denying *The Family of Man*'s middlebrow
> aesthetics or the facts of racial, sexual, and political repression
> in 1950s America, I want to scrape away several decades' worth of
> critical disdain and illuminate a deeply democratic, even utopian,
> impulse that drove the show and much of the early audience response
> to it. I particularly want to revisit the antiauthoritarian politics
> behind its design and the modes of attention it solicited from
> visitors." (Turner 2012, p. 57)

The article's central historical argument is that *The Family of
Man*'s installation logic — Paul Rudolph's hanging, the variety of
heights and angles, the absence of a single forced path — was a
deliberate inheritance from Herbert Bayer's 1942 *Road to Victory*
(also Steichen-curated at MoMA) and, through Bayer, from a Bauhaus
exhibition tradition that Turner traces back to the 1930 Werkbund
Paris exposition. The Bauhaus inheritance is, on Turner's reading,
specifically anti-fascist:

> "Within Bayer's extended field of vision, however, fragments of
> media surrounded viewers — viewers who in turn reached out to the
> images they saw, selecting, arranging, and integrating them in
> their minds into their own individual gestalts. In Bayer's work,
> viewers took charge of both the viewing process and the
> construction of their psyches. As a result, at least in theory,
> viewers became more independent and psychologically whole."
> (Turner 2012, p. 68)

That technique, Turner argues, was carried forward into the design of
*The Family of Man*'s second-floor installation at MoMA in 1955, with
its varied heights, hanging panels, ceiling-mounted images, and the
hydrogen-bomb transparency that visitors had to confront on the way
out. The intellectual framework around that design is, on Turner's
reading, a synthesis of Frankfurt School analysis of authoritarianism
(Horkheimer's *Eclipse of Reason*, Adorno et al.'s *Authoritarian
Personality*), Committee for National Morale work of the early 1940s
(Allport, Lasswell, Mead, Bateson, Benedict), and the postwar
"democratic personality" literature (Lasswell 1951). The exhibition,
on this account, was attempting to *produce* a particular kind of
viewer — not a "passive audience" being told a "single message," but
an audience practising what Lasswell had called "the open as against
a closed ego."

Turner's thesis is therefore, as he states it in the article's
conclusion, exactly the inverse of the 1957 / 1981 reading on the
question of audience:

> "By today's standards, such an innovation might seem mild, even
> trivial. From a time in which digital screens bombard us with images
> from every conceivable angle and in places as diverse as football
> stadiums, airplanes, and bedrooms, it is difficult to imagine how
> important Bayer's new strategy actually was."
> (Turner 2012, p. 68)

> "*The Family of Man* thus modeled a more diverse and tolerant
> society, but also a society whose members had adjusted themselves to
> an array of opportunities chosen on their behalf by those in power.
> In comparison to fascist alternatives, the world brought to life at
> the Museum of Modern Art in 1955 must have looked enormously
> individualistic, varied, and free. But even as it challenged the
> hierarchies of totalitarianism, the exhibition modeled the
> emergence of a society whose citizens were to manage themselves in
> terms set by the systems within which they lived — and by the
> experts who developed those systems."
> (Turner 2012, p. 84)

Turner is therefore explicit that his reading is **not** a
rehabilitation in the sense of denying the show's complicity:

> "Such invitations to construct identities in terms set by the media
> around us have become commonplace today. And they point to a
> paradox: even as *The Family of Man* championed a far more open,
> tolerant, and diverse society than we remember, it also helped
> deliver us into a world in which media constantly ask us to manage
> ourselves in terms set by faraway others." (Turner 2012, p. 58)

The closest the article comes to a verdict-level summary is the final
paragraph's "holistic, individualistic, utopian vision that would
animate the countercultural outbursts to come" alongside its role in
"pioneer[ing] postmodern modes of mediated authority" (Turner 2012,
p. 84). The two phrases must be cited together; quoting only the
first is the predictable misreading of Turner as a defender of the
show.

The article's footnotes are also informative for the reception thread.
Turner cites Barthes, Phillips ("The Judgment Seat of Photography,"
*October* 22, 1982), John Berger (*About Looking*, 1980), Solomon-Godeau,
and Sekula ("The Traffic in Photographs," *Art Journal* 41:1, 1981) as
the canonical critical voices he is engaging with; Sandeen 1995, Stimson
2006, Bonnie Brennen and Hanno Hardt (eds.) 1999, and Berlier's chapter
in that volume as the more nuanced or sympathetic precedents; and
Staniszewski's *Power of Display* (MIT, 1998) and Ribalta 2008 (MACBA)
as the architectural-history precedents (Turner 2012, p. 56, n. 2;
p. 61, n. 11).

The honest summary: **Turner 2013 is the canonical 2010s scholarly

## 4. The geographic widening: James, Sandeen-2015, Nakamori

The 2010s see the strongest expansion of *Family of Man*'s reception
geography since Sandeen 1995. Three peer-reviewed studies define the
expansion.

**Sarah E. James 2012**, "A Post-Fascist Family of Man? Cold War
Humanism, Democracy and Photography in Germany," *Oxford Art Journal*
35, no. 3 (December 2012): 315–336 [src-james-2012-post-fascist]. The
article's stated aim, on its abstract — "rehistoricising and
repoliticising *The Family of Man* in relation to issues of humanism
and democracy in a divided Germany of the 1950s and 1960s" —
positions it as a parallel project to Turner's, but on the European
side and with a different emphasis: the post-Holocaust, post-fascist
discursive context within which the West Berlin opening of the
European tour did its work. The article also addresses Karl Pawek's
1964 *Was ist der Mensch?* counter-exhibition. The body text was
**not** consulted in this round; the abstract and search-snippet
verbatim are recorded in the source file.

**Eric Sandeen 2015**, "*The Family of Man* in Guatemala," *Visual
Studies* 30, no. 2 (2015): pp. unknown this round
[src-sandeen-2015-guatemala], in the special issue "Cold War Visual
Alliances" guest-edited by Sarah Bassnett, Andrea Noble, and Thy Phu
[src-visual-studies-2015-cold-war]. Sandeen's argument, on the
search-snippet verbatim recorded in the source file (Tandfonline
abstract page returned 403 on direct fetch this round, 2026-05-09):
"Placing the exhibition in Guatemala reoriented the show's Cold War
geography and challenged the limits of Steichen's humanism.
Steichen's orientation was East–West, his purpose to break down what
he considered to be a nihilistic binary between the United States and
the Soviet Union. A North–South axis brings with it issues of
hereditary, festering colonialism and comprehensive exploitation of
indigenous populations." The Guatemala City showing took place at
the Palacio Protocolo, 24 August – 18 September 1955, fourteen months
after the 1954 CIA-backed coup against Árbenz — a juxtaposition
Sandeen treats as decisive. This is a notable shift in Sandeen's own
position: where his 1995 book reads the show in the East–West
framework that Steichen and the USIA themselves used, his 2015
article opens the North–South question that the Anglophone reception
literature had largely left to Latin American historians.

**Yasufumi Nakamori (Graham Foundation grant project)**
[src-nakamori-graham-fom-tokyo]: "*The Family of Man* (1956) in Tokyo,
Japan: Kenzo Tange's Pursuit of the Notions of Humanity / Humanism
through His Exhibition Design." The Graham Foundation grant record
documents Nakamori's research on Kenzo Tange's installation design for
the 1956 Takashimaya Department Store presentation and the six other
Japanese cities on the tour. As of this round (2026-05-09), no
single resulting publication is confirmed; the project is documented
through the grant record only and the Graham Foundation page itself
was not directly fetched (entries in `sources/2010s/` carry the search-
snippet verbatim only). The frame is significant for the world-tour
account: where the Anglophone literature on Tokyo's reception is thin,
Nakamori's project reads the Tokyo showing as the first major
exhibition-design commission of one of the twentieth century's most
important architects, with Bauhaus connections that parallel Turner's
Bayer / Steichen genealogy in the New York installation.

These three studies do not displace each other; together they redraw
the reception map. In Sandeen-1995's day a Berlin chapter, a
Guatemala chapter, and a Kenzo-Tange Tokyo chapter were not yet
distinct objects of Anglophone scholarship. By the end of the 2010s
they are.

A fourth axis — the African / South African chapter — is opened in
parallel in this decade by Patricia Hayes, Darren Newbury, and others.
The most substantial 2010s anchor for that strand is Okwui Enwezor and
Rory Bester, eds., *Rise and Fall of Apartheid: Photography and the
Bureaucracy of Everyday Life* (ICP / DelMonico Books / Prestel, 2013)
[src-enwezor-bester-2013-rise-fall-apartheid]. The catalog itself is
not confirmed to address *Family of Man* directly (the ICP exhibition
page fetched 2026-04-30 makes no mention of Steichen); it functions
in the 2010s reception thread as the major scholarly anchor on the
photographic context within which the exhibition's August 1958
Johannesburg showing took place. The detailed account of Newbury's
own dedicated work on *Family of Man* in Africa belongs in the 2020s
essay (`src-newbury-2024-cold-war-photographic-diplomacy`,
`src-newbury-2024-african-looks-at-america`), but the trajectory
begins here.

## 5. The 2018 essay collection: *The Family of Man Revisited*

The decade closes with the most ambitious book-length critical
reassessment of *The Family of Man* since Sandeen 1995: Gerd Hurm,
Anke Reitz, and Shamoon Zamir, eds., *The Family of Man Revisited:
Photography in a Global Age* (London: I.B. Tauris, 2018), 336 pp.,
ISBN 978-1-78453-967-2 [src-hurm-reitz-zamir-2018]. The CNA
Luxembourg documentation page for the volume was retrieved via
`curl` on 2026-05-09 and is the source for both the contributor list
and the editorial framing.

The contributor list, verbatim from the CNA documentation page
(fetched 2026-05-09): "Winfried Fluck, Ulrike Gehring, Gerd Hurm,
Martin Jay, Miles Orvell, Anke Reitz, Eric Sandeen, Kerstin Schmidt,
Werner Sollors and Shamoon Zamir." The presence of both Sandeen
(continuing the historical-contextual line opened in 1995) and Martin
Jay (the leading Anglophone historian of the Frankfurt School and
"downcast eyes" / vision-theory writer) is the volume's clearest
statement of its scholarly geography.

The editorial framing, verbatim from the same page:

> "*The Family of Man: Photography in a Global Age* revises the
> critical debate about *The Family of Man*, challenging in particular
> the legacy of Roland Barthes's influential account of the
> exhibition. The expert contributors explore new contexts for
> understanding Steichen's work and they undertake radically new
> analyses of the formal dynamics of the exhibition."
> (CNA documentation page, fetched 2026-05-09)

Three primary documents are presented in the volume "for the first
time in English," per the same page: "Commentaries by critical
theorist Max Horkheimer and novelist Wolfgang Koeppen, a letter from
photographer August Sander, and a poetic sequence on the images by
Polish poet Witold Wirpsza." A separate Munich audience-response
section is also flagged: "A detailed survey of audience responses in
Munich from 1955 allows a rare glimpse of what visitors thought about
the exhibition."

The body text of the essay collection was **not** consulted this
round; the I.B. Tauris / Bloomsbury and Routledge / T&F catalog DOIs
attempted on 2026-04-30 were both denied, and were not re-attempted on
2026-05-09. The chapter-level claims of Sandeen, Jay, Sollors, Fluck,
or any other contributor are therefore **not** asserted in this essay.

The volume is, however, the institutional 2010s closer in two ways
that are confirmable from the CNA page alone. First, the volume is
hosted on the CNA Luxembourg documentation portal alongside the
Clervaux collection — Anke Reitz, one of the three editors, appears
in CNA materials in connection with the Steichen Collections (her
exact CNA role and title are NOT confirmed from the documentation
page fetched 2026-05-09, which lists her as co-editor only) — which
means that the same institution holding the UNESCO-inscribed
collection is associated with the decade's major scholarly companion
to it. This is similar to the pattern observed for the 2000s with
Back & Schmidt-Linsenhoff 2004 (Jean Back, the Luxembourg co-editor,
likewise carrying a CNA institutional connection that was not
re-fetched in this round). Second, the editors'
explicit framing is "challenging in particular the legacy of Roland
Barthes's influential account of the exhibition" — not, notably,
the *October* / Sekula axis, which is left without rebuttal in the
quoted blurb. This is consistent with Turner 2012's own focus on
Barthes-via-Phillips and on the reception's "middlebrow" characterisation
rather than on Sekula's specifically political reading.

## 6. The Clervaux restoration, September 2010 – July 2013

The institutional event of the 2010s for *The Family of Man*'s
afterlife is the closure-and-restoration period at Clervaux Castle.
The CNA collections page (fetched 2026-04-29 and re-confirmed via
curl 2026-05-09) documents the dates and the restoration team
[src-cna-collections-eng-family-of-man]. The page reads, verbatim:

> "**September 2010** — Closure of the exhibition for renovation
> purposes."
> "**July 2013** — Reopening following renovation of exhibition rooms
> and restoration of photographs."
> "A second restoration period followed the closure of the museum in
> the years 2010-2013. Recent developments in the science and tools
> used in photography restoration enabled a state-of-the-art analysis
> and treatment of the photographs to be carried out. A lot of the
> damage noted was able to be dealt with thanks to restoration
> treatments such as cleaning, consolidation and retouching."
> "The restoration campaign has been led in collaboration with the
> Studio Berselli from Milan, Italy (Silvia Berselli, Roberta
> Piantavigna, Francesca Vantellini, Isabel Dimas)."
> "the exhibition rooms featuring a very sober architecture conceived
> by designer Nathalie Jacoby (NJOY)."

Three observations belong on the reception thread.

First, the 2010–2013 restoration is the second major restoration
campaign on the inscribed collection — the first having taken place
in the 1990s, also in collaboration with Studio Berselli. Inscription
on the UNESCO Memory of the World register (2003) does not require
restoration; the choice to undertake a second restoration in the
decade after inscription is institutional, not regulatory.

Second, the architectural redesign of the exhibition rooms by
Nathalie Jacoby (NJOY) means that the post-2013 Clervaux installation
is not a reconstruction of any of the 1955 / 1956 / 1958 USIA
installations. The CNA-Luxembourg display environment is its own
twenty-first-century curatorial object. This matters for the
reception thread because Turner's argument about the 1955 Rudolph
hanging — varied heights, ceiling-mounted images, the "extended field
of vision" — is not by itself a description of the present-day
visitor experience at Clervaux. Visitors today see the same prints in
a different installation language.

Third, the restoration period overlaps with the publication of Turner
2012, Turner 2013, James 2012, Sandeen 2015, and the 2015 *Visual
Studies* "Cold War Visual Alliances" special issue. The Clervaux
museum was closed during the years that produced the densest cluster
of *Family of Man* scholarship since the 1980s. Whether this is
coincidence or whether the closure created an opening for
re-evaluation is not a question this essay can settle.

## 7. The 60th anniversary, 2015

The 60th-anniversary moment in 2015 is observable in the reception
thread primarily through the *Visual Studies* "Cold War Visual

[src-visual-studies-2015-cold-war], which contains Sandeen's

The Clervaux reopening (July 2013) preceded the 60th anniversary by

(`src-cna-education`) and the CNA Steichen biographical page

biographical page describes Steichen's MoMA tenure and *Family of

introduction, with the addition of the Luxembourg-emigration

Other 60th-anniversary-period scholarly events identified in the
project so far — the I.B. Tauris essay collection (2018) originating

## 9. Where the 2010s reading sits relative to earlier decades

The 2010s reading is the first decade since the 1980s in which a
major scholar (Turner) sets out to *defend* — with explicit
qualifications — the political project of the exhibition against the
Barthes / Phillips / Sekula consensus. Two qualifications belong on
that observation.

A note on this framing. The phrase "first decade since the 1980s" is
meant in a narrow sense: the first decade in which a scholar engages
directly and at book length with the *October*-axis political critique
on its own terms (audience-experience, installation logic, the show's
"design grammar") and offers a counter-reading at that level. Sandeen
1995 is also a recovery reading, but its register is
historical-contextual reconstruction rather than a head-on rebuttal of
Phillips / Sekula. Stimson 2006 is sympathetic to the show but reads
it as one term in a comparative argument about mass-photographic
objects (alongside Frank's *The Americans* and the Bechers) rather
than as the primary object of defence. Turner's distinctive
contribution is to take Barthes / Phillips / Sekula as the explicit
interlocutors and to argue that they have the show's *intent and
audience experience* wrong. Readers who would characterise Sandeen
1995 or Stimson 2006 as the relevant precedent are not wrong; the
phrasing here flags the methodological difference rather than denying
the precedent.

First, Turner's defence is not a reversal of the *October* axis at
the level of fact. He grants the show's "middlebrow aesthetics," the
"facts of racial, sexual, and political repression in 1950s America,"
and the show's role in pioneering "postmodern modes of mediated
authority" (Turner 2012, pp. 57, 84). The defence operates at the
level of *intent and audience experience*: Turner's claim is that the
show was attempting to do something different from what its critics
charge it with — to produce democratic personalities through an
anti-fascist installation logic — and that the early reviewers
(Edward Alden Jewell in *NYT* 1942 on *Road to Victory*; Barbara
Morgan in *Aperture* 1955 on *The Family of Man*) recognised this
specific claim about the audience-as-co-author. The 1957 / 1981 /
1982 critical tradition, on Turner's reading, characterises the show
in terms of its message and underplays its design.

Second, Turner's reading does not render the *October*-axis critique
moot. The Phillips / Sekula reading — that the show is ideologically
continuous with US Cold War cultural diplomacy and aesthetically
continuous with MoMA's institutional canonisation of an
authoritative photographic gaze — is a reading of the exhibition's
*function* in the field of cultural diplomacy and museum institutional
politics. Turner's reading is a reading of the exhibition's
*intentional design and audience invitation*. Both can be true at
once, and Turner himself states this when he closes with the
"paradox" passage on p. 58: even as the show "championed a far more
open, tolerant, and diverse society than we remember, it also helped
deliver us into a world in which media constantly ask us to manage
ourselves in terms set by faraway others." The 2010s reception is
best characterised as a decade that holds these two readings in
tension rather than choosing between them.

The geographic widening (James, Sandeen-2015, Nakamori, the
Newbury-Africa thread that begins here and extends into the 2020s)
adds a third register that neither the *October*-axis critique nor
Turner's defence had fully developed. The question what *Family of
Man* did, specifically, in West Berlin, Guatemala City, Tokyo,
Johannesburg, or Nagasaki — venue by venue, with the local press,
local curatorial choices, and local political context all on the
record — is the 2010s' most durable methodological contribution.
