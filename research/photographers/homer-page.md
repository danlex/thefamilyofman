# Homer Page (1918–1985)

Homer Page was an American documentary photographer best known for the New York street photographs he made during a 1949 Guggenheim Fellowship — though, by plate count, his largest single body of work in print is the ten plates that Edward Steichen selected for *The Family of Man* in 1955. The Wikipedia article (fetched 2026-05-09) opens its lead "**Homer Page** (1918–1985) was an American documentary photographer whose most famous photographs were taken in New York City in 1949–1950, after he received a grant from the Guggenheim Foundation" (src-wikipedia-homer-page-pointer). The 1918 / 1985 year-level dates are corroborated by the Wikipedia article's category metadata ("1918 births", "1985 deaths", "20th-century American photographers"). ## Career

The early career arc is verbatim per Wikipedia (src-wikipedia-homer-page-pointer): "Page was born in Oakland, California, and studied art and social psychology at the University of California, graduating in 1940. He worked in the shipyards in the Oakland-San Francisco Bay Area during World War II. His neighbor and later his mentor, photographer Dorothea Lange, encouraged him to take up photography in 1944. By 1947, he was featured in a major show at the Museum of Modern Art in New York."

The Lange-Page mentorship is the single most important biographical link in the FoM curatorial pathway. Lange was already represented by nine plates in FoM (per `pher-dorothea-lange` row, verified 2026-05-07) and is documented in the Daily Art Magazine 70th-anniversary article (`src-west-2025-dailyart-lange-fom`, in repo, `verified: false`) as having played a curatorial role helping Steichen shape FoM. The 1944 mentorship and the shared FSA-adjacent Bay Area documentary milieu situate Page in Lange's professional circle at exactly the period when she was exerting curatorial influence on FoM's American documentary tier.

The Guggenheim period and FoM inclusion are the centerpiece of the Wikipedia article's narrative: "Page received a Guggenheim Fellowship in 1949 and spent a year documenting modern urban culture, primarily by photographing people on the streets of New York City. Most of his subjects appear unaware of his presence. Some of Page's photographs were included in Edward Steichen's landmark *Family of Man* exhibition at the Museum of Modern Art in 1955. His photographs appeared in publications including Harper's Magazine, and books such as *The Little World of Laos*. In 1966, he published a collection of his photographs, titled *Puerto Rico: The Quiet Revolution*" (src-wikipedia-homer-page-pointer). Subsequent magazine work for the World Health Organization (1957–1960) carried Page to Latin America, Asia, and Africa to photograph public-health subjects (yaws, trachoma, rural health).

## Posthumous rediscovery

Page's work was largely forgotten by the time of his 1985 death. Wikipedia (verbatim): "The bulk of Page's career was spent as a magazine photographer, and, as a result, few of his photographs were in private hands and his work was largely forgotten by the time of his death in 1985, at the age of 67." His rediscovery is dated to the mid-1990s by the same source: "Keith F. Davis, Curator of Photography at the Nelson-Atkins Museum of Art, while researching and writing the first edition of *An American Century of Photography*, published in 1995, became aware of Page's non-magazine work. Davis searched for Page's photographs, leading to the discovery of a 'lost' photographic treasure. After negotiations with the Page estate, Nelson-Atkins purchased about 100 prints for the museum's Hallmark Photographic Collection, including many that were one of a kind." Davis's 2009 Yale University Press monograph *Homer Page: The Guggenheim Year, New York, 1949–50* (cited in the Wikipedia article's external links but NOT consulted at full text in this round) is the canonical Tier-2 reference for promoting these biographical claims.

## Connection to *The Family of Man*

Homer Page is represented by ten plates in *The Family of Man* (count verified by strict-match grep against `data/photographs.csv` 2026-05-09): photo-0097, photo-0120, photo-0150, photo-0151, photo-0162, photo-0168, photo-0278, photo-0374, photo-0399 (the only non-USA plate, set in South Africa), and photo-0484. This places him among the **top contributors to FoM by plate count** (after "Photographer unknown" at 14, Wayne Miller at 12, Henri Cartier-Bresson at 11; Homer Page is at 10, tied or near-tied with Dorothea Lange at 9). Yet Page is among the least-discussed FoM photographers in the secondary literature — a striking gap given the volume of his contribution.
