# Henri Cartier-Bresson (1908–2004)

Henri Cartier-Bresson was a French photographer and a co-founder of Magnum Photos. His eleven plates make him one of the most heavily represented contributors to *The Family of Man* — by strict-match grep against `data/photographs.csv` (this session) he is the third-largest individual contributor by plate count (after "Photographer unknown" at 14 and Wayne Miller at 12, equal-tied with no other named photographer at 11). All eleven plates are credited to the Magnum cooperative agency in the MoMA Master Checklist. Birth and death years and nationality are anchored at year-level by three Tier-1 institutional pages re-fetched live this session: the ICP archive page (live fetch 2026-05-30 into `.scratch/hcb-icp-archive.html`; HTTP 200, 55,421 bytes; in-repo `src-icp-cartier-bresson-archive`) opens "Cartier-Bresson was born in Chanteloup, and studied literature at Cambridge University in 1928-29"; the Magnum profile (live fetch 2026-05-30 into `.scratch/hcb-magnum-profile.html`; HTTP 200, 135,628 bytes; in-repo `src-magnum-photographer-bios` covering Magnum's per-member URL pattern) opens "b. 1908 d. 2004 French Founding member"; the Fondation Henri Cartier-Bresson biographical chronology (live fetch 2026-05-30 into `.scratch/hcb-fondation-biography.html`; HTTP 200, 40,156 bytes; not yet registered in `sources/`, flagged here as candidate `src-fondation-hcb-biography`) opens its 1908 entry verbatim "Born on August 22nd in Chanteloup, Seine-et-Marne. Educated at the Lycée Condorcet, Paris." The Fondation HCB page (the photographer's own estate institution) is the **first Tier-1 anchor for the day-month birth and death tokens** consulted in any round; its 1908 entry gives "August 22nd" and its 2004 entry gives "Henri Cartier-Bresson dies peacefully in Montjustin, Provence on August 3rd." These day-month tokens previously had pointer-only Tier-3 status from `src-wikipedia-cartier-bresson-pointer`; they are now anchored to a Tier-1 estate source.

This file is one of the issue-#184 per-photographer deep-research files for the exhibition's most-represented contributors. It deepens the prior twenty-line biography with a 1955-selection analysis, a pre-1955 career arc, a per-plate treatment of all eleven plates, and a survey of how the critical literature has read his FoM contribution.

## 1955 selection — how Cartier-Bresson's eleven plates entered *The Family of Man*

Cartier-Bresson is represented by exactly **eleven** plates in *The Family of Man* (count verified this session by strict-match grep `grep '"Henri Cartier-Bresson"' data/photographs.csv | wc -l` → 11, and independently by the pre-flight `python3 scripts/photographer_anchors.py "Henri Cartier-Bresson"` this session). Their entry into the exhibition is documented at the plate level by the MoMA Master Checklist (`src-moma-exh-0569-master-checklist`, Tier-1, in-repo); **no source consulted this round documents the pathway by which any individual plate was solicited or selected.** Neither the ICP archive page, the Magnum profile, nor the Fondation HCB biographical chronology — all live-fetched this session — mentions *The Family of Man* or Edward Steichen by name in their biographical text. The Fondation chronology lists Cartier-Bresson's 1955 activity as "First exhibition in France at the Pavillon de Marsan in the Louvre. Tériade publishes *Les Européens* (cover illustrated by Miró)" — the Pavillon de Marsan show and the Tériade publication are noted; *The Family of Man* is not.

**The Magnum pathway.** All eleven Cartier-Bresson plates carry the Magnum agency credit in the MoMA Master Checklist (verbatim: "Magnum" or, for #190, the alternate spelling "MAGNUM"; verified this session against the eleven plate-lines in `sources/1950s/moma-exh-0569-master-checklist.md` at lines 70, 234, 317, 423, 464, 565, 570, 647, 711, 776, 778). The Fondation chronology gives the Magnum founding verbatim under 1947: "Creates the cooperative agency Magnum Photos with Robert Capa, David Seymour (Chim), William Vandivert, and George Rodger" (the full five-name founding roster). The ICP page gives a partial four-name list — "In 1947 he co-founded the Magnum agency with Robert Capa, Chim (David Seymour), and George Rodger" — omitting Vandivert; the Magnum profile gives the same five-name roster as the Fondation page. The Cartier-Bresson cluster of eleven Magnum-credited plates is the largest single-photographer contribution channelled through Magnum in FoM, ahead of Robert Capa (5 Magnum-credited plates per the `pher-robert-capa` row), Ernst Haas (6 Magnum-credited plates per the `pher-ernst-haas` row), George Rodger (2 plates per the `pher-george-rodger` row), and the other Magnum members in the show. **What this evidence does not support:** any claim that Cartier-Bresson himself negotiated plate selection with Steichen, or that Magnum acted as a co-curator. No correspondence between Cartier-Bresson and Steichen, and no statement by Cartier-Bresson about FoM specifically, is in any source consulted this round.

**A documented prior MoMA relationship — but not via Steichen.** The Fondation chronology records under 1947: "Exhibition *Photographs by Henri Cartier-Bresson* at the Museum of Modern Art in New York. Creates the cooperative agency Magnum Photos…" The 1947 MoMA Cartier-Bresson solo exhibition predates Steichen's appointment as MoMA's Director of the Department of Photography (commonly dated 1947 but with Steichen's tenure conventionally beginning July 1947; **the specific MoMA curator of the 1947 Cartier-Bresson exhibition is not stated in any source consulted this round**). The 1947 show predates *The Family of Man* by eight years. A documented post-1955 second MoMA Cartier-Bresson exhibition follows in the Fondation chronology under 1987: "Photographic exhibition *The Early Work* at The Museum of Modern Art, New York." Neither the 1947 nor the 1987 entry is connected to Steichen or to FoM in the Fondation text; both are listed as standalone MoMA shows. The institutional fact that Cartier-Bresson had a solo MoMA show eight years before FoM is recorded; any inference about how that prior institutional relationship affected his FoM inclusion is structural, not attested.

**Steichen relationship.** The Tier-1 biographical pages (ICP, Magnum profile, Fondation chronology — all consulted this session) **make no reference to Edward Steichen by name.** No correspondence, no statement, and no documented working relationship between the two photographers is in any source consulted this round. Steichen at MoMA from 1947 onward was Cartier-Bresson's institutional curator-of-record for the 1947 solo show only by the structural fact that MoMA's photography department was under Steichen's direction in that year — **no source consulted this round names Steichen as the curator of the 1947 show.** Any sharper claim about a Steichen–Cartier-Bresson relationship would require Cartier-Bresson's correspondence (held at the Fondation HCB) or a Tier-1/2 biography not consulted here.

**Sequence position and weighting.** Cartier-Bresson's eleven plates are distributed across nine of the exhibition's checklist sections: #31 (Section 3 Marriage), #128 (Section 14 Land), #190 (Section 17 Woman's Work), #259 (Section 23 Food), #288 (Section 25 Relationships), #366 (Section 28 Religious Expression), #371 (Section 28 Religious Expression), #409 (Section 34 Revolt), #442 (Section 38 Government), #481 and #483 (Section 42 Childhood Magic). No other photographer appears across so wide a section range. This breadth — combined with the eleven-plate count — gives Cartier-Bresson a structural role distributing the Magnum aesthetic across the entire arc of the exhibition rather than clustering in one or two sections.

## Career arc, 1908–1955

The run-up to FoM is anchored at Tier-1 across three live-fetched pages: the ICP archive page (live fetch 2026-05-30), the Magnum profile (live fetch 2026-05-30), and the Fondation HCB biographical chronology (live fetch 2026-05-30 — the most detailed, with year-by-year entries). Where the three diverge, both renderings are preserved. Post-1955 activity is out of the FoM run-up and is noted briefly at the end for completeness.

**Chanteloup beginnings and Paris education (1908–1929).** Fondation, 1908 entry (verbatim, live fetch this session): "Born on August 22nd in Chanteloup, Seine-et-Marne. Educated at the Lycée Condorcet, Paris." ICP (verbatim): "Cartier-Bresson was born in Chanteloup, and studied literature at Cambridge University in 1928-29." The Fondation chronology adds the 1926 entry verbatim: "Studies painting under André Lhote. Takes his first photographs." The Lhote / painting period is on the Fondation page only; ICP gives the Cambridge literature year only; the Magnum profile gives no education details. The three sources thus agree on Chanteloup birth and Paris-area education, with the Cambridge 1928–29 detail anchored by ICP and the Lhote painting study anchored by the Fondation.

**Ivory Coast, the Leica, and the surrealist circle (1930–1933).** Fondation, 1930 entry (verbatim): "Spends almost one year in the Ivory Coast." 1931 entry (verbatim): "Discovers a photograph by Martin Munkácsi in the arts magazine *Arts et Métiers Graphiques* and decides to focus on photography." 1931–32 entry (verbatim): "Buys his first Leica in Paris and travels across Europe with his friends Leonor Fini and Pieyre de Mandiargues. First publications in *Voilà* and *Photographies*." ICP gives the same period as: "He began photographing in 1931 and purchased a Leica in 1933." The Fondation thus dates the Leica purchase to 1931–32 while ICP dates it to 1933. The two are not necessarily contradictory if the 1931–32 entry covers acquisition and the 1933 ICP date covers a different camera or a definitive replacement, but as fetched this session they are recorded as a year-discrepancy: 1931–32 (Fondation) vs. 1933 (ICP). The Magnum profile gives only "In 1932, after spending a year in the Ivory Coast, he discovered the Leica, his camera of choice thereafter." Magnum dates the Leica encounter to 1932, midway between the Fondation 1931–32 and ICP 1933 windows. The discrepancy is preserved, not adjudicated.

**Mexico ethnographic expedition and the New York / Paul Strand encounter (1934–1935).** Fondation, 1934 entry (verbatim): "Goes to Mexico with an ethnographic expedition team. The mission fails, but he decides to stay." 1935 entry (verbatim): "Exhibits with Manuel Alvarez Bravo at the Palacio de Bellas Artes, Mexico and one month later at the Julien Levy Gallery with Walker Evans and Manuel Alvarez-Bravo. Spends some time in the USA, where he discovers filmmaking with Paul Strand and Nykino Group." ICP gives the same period as: "He joined an ethnographic expedition to Mexico in 1934, and in 1935 studied cinematography with Paul Strand." The Mexico-and-Strand sequence is consistent across the two Tier-1 pages; the 1933 Julien Levy first New York exhibition (Fondation) is consistent with ICP's note that the Strand encounter came after the U.S. arrival. The Magnum profile gives a different framing — "He had his first exhibition at the Julien Levy Gallery in New York in 1933" — agreeing on 1933 for Julien Levy but omitting Mexico and Strand entirely. The Magnum 1933 Julien Levy date matches the Fondation 1933 entry verbatim.

**Renoir film assistant and Spanish Republican documentary work (1936–1939).** Fondation, 1936 entry (verbatim): "Works as second assistant to Jean Renoir on *Une partie de campagne* (*A Day in the Country*) and *La vie est à nous*." 1937 entry (verbatim): "Directs two documentaries: *Victoire de la vie* (*Return to Life*) on the medical care of Republican Spain and *With the Abraham Lincoln Brigade,* on the American soldiers' life during the war. Photographic report on the coronation of George VI published in the newspaper *Ce Soir.*" 1938 entry: "Directs, upon request by the Spanish Secours Populaire, a documentary about the Spanish Civil War, *L'Espagne vivra*." 1939 entry: "Joins Jacques Becker and André Zvoboda as an assistant on Jean Renoir's *La Règle du jeu* (*The Rules of the Game*)." ICP (verbatim): "He assisted Jean Renoir in 1936 and 1939, and made his own documentary, Return to Life, in 1937." The Spanish Republican documentary work is anchored on the Fondation page; ICP gives only the *Return to Life* title without the Republican-Spain context. The Magnum profile gives only "He later made films with Jean Renoir" without dates or titles.

**Internment, escape, and the French underground (1940–1945).** Fondation, 1940 entry (verbatim): "Joins the 'Film and Photography' unit of the Third Army. Taken prisoner by the Germans on June 23rd." 1943 entry (verbatim): "After two failed attempts, successfully escapes on his third attempt in February 1943. Works for MNPGD, a secret organization created to help prisoners and escapees." 1944 entry (verbatim): "Takes a series of photographic portraits of writers and artists for Editions Braun (Henri Matisse, Pablo Picasso, Georges Braque, Pierre Bonnard, Paul Claudel, Georges Rouault)." 1945 entry (verbatim): "Directs *Le Retour* (*The Return*), a documentary on the repatriation of prisoners of war and detainees." ICP (verbatim): "He was drafted into the film and photo unit of the French army in 1940 and was taken prisoner by the Germans that same year. After three years of imprisonment he escaped and began working for the French underground. In 1943 he made series of portraits of artists, including Matisse, Bonnard, and Braque. Through 1944 and 1945, Cartier-Bresson photographed the occupation of France and its liberation." Magnum (verbatim): "Taken prisoner of war in 1940, he escaped on his third attempt in 1943 and subsequently joined an underground organization to assist prisoners and escapees. In 1945, he photographed the Liberation of Paris with a group of professional journalists, then filmed the documentary *Le Retour (The Return)*." The three sources agree on the 1940 capture, three-year internment, and 1943 escape; ICP dates the artist portraits to 1943, the Fondation to 1944. The wartime escape narrative is the single most thoroughly cross-anchored episode in the pre-FoM career.

**The 1947 MoMA exhibition and the Magnum founding (1946–1947).** Fondation, 1946–47 entry (verbatim): "Spends over a year in the USA. Upon *Harper's Bazaar* request, he travels for a few months with John Malcolm Brinnin to photograph artists and writers." Fondation, 1947 entry (verbatim): "Exhibition *Photographs by Henri Cartier-Bresson* at the Museum of Modern Art in New York. Creates the cooperative agency Magnum Photos with Robert Capa, David Seymour (Chim), William Vandivert, and George Rodger." Magnum (verbatim): "In 1947, with Robert Capa, George Rodger, David 'Chim' Seymour, and William Vandivert, he founded Magnum Photos." ICP (verbatim): "In 1947 he co-founded the Magnum agency with Robert Capa, Chim (David Seymour), and George Rodger." Two of three Tier-1 pages give the full five-name founding roster; ICP gives a four-name roster omitting William Vandivert. The 1947 MoMA solo exhibition is anchored only on the Fondation page (the Magnum and ICP texts read this session do not mention it); it is the structural fact that connects Cartier-Bresson to MoMA before *The Family of Man*.

**The Far East period (1948–1950) — the period that produced most of FoM's Asian plates.** Fondation, 1948–50 entry (verbatim, live fetch this session): "Spends three years in the Far East: in India for the death of Gandhi, in China for the last six months of the Kuomintang and the first six months of the People's Republic, and in Indonesia for its independence. His photographs are published all over the world." Magnum (verbatim): "After three years spent traveling in the East, he returned to Europe in 1952…" The Magnum profile names no specific countries for the Far East period; the Fondation is the load-bearing anchor for the India/Gandhi, China/Kuomintang–PRC transition, and Indonesia/independence framing. ICP (read this session): no parallel China/India/Indonesia year detail on the live-fetched ICP body. **This is the most directly load-bearing pre-FoM career anchor for the FoM Asian plates.** Six of Cartier-Bresson's eleven FoM plates are set in Asia (Indonesia, Bali, Kashmir, China, Iran, Java); all six are consistent with the documented 1948–50 Far East travel, though no source consulted this round identifies any specific FoM plate as a known image from that period. The 1948 Gandhi-funeral coverage, the 1948–49 Beijing/Shanghai work, and the Indonesia-independence work are the famous Cartier-Bresson bodies of work from this period; **whether any FoM plate is one of these famous images is not established in any source consulted this round** (see "The plates" below for the per-plate caution).

**The 1952 publication of *Images à la Sauvette / The Decisive Moment*.** Fondation, 1952 entry (verbatim): "His first book, *Images à la Sauvette*, with its cover by Matisse, is published by Tériade. First exhibition in England, *Photographs by Henri Cartier-Bresson* at the Institute of Contemporary Arts." Magnum (verbatim): "After three years spent traveling in the East, he returned to Europe in 1952, where he published his first book, *Images à la Sauvette* (published in English as *The Decisive Moment*)." ICP (re-read this session) does not give a 1952 anchor for the book; the prior in-repo summary of ICP renders the book's year as 1952 in the source-file `Notes`. The 1952 publication is the foundational text of the "decisive moment" doctrine; that doctrine is named once in the Magnum bio's opening narrative (read this session in the prior in-repo `src-magnum-photographer-bios` summary) as the phrase by which Cartier-Bresson's practice is characterised, and is referenced verbatim on the ICP page (live fetch this session): "His photographs may be summed up through a phrase of his own: 'the decisive moment,' the magical instant when the world falls into apparent order and meaning, and may be apprehended by a gifted photographer."

**1953–1955: prizes, the USSR access, and the Pavillon de Marsan exhibition.** Fondation, 1953 entry (verbatim): "Travels throughout Europe for *Holiday*." 1954 entry (verbatim): "Publication by Robert Delpire of his books *Les Danses à Bali* and *D'une Chine à l'autre,* marking the beginning of a long collaboration with Delpire. He is the first photographer allowed in the USSR since the death of Stalin." 1955 entry (verbatim): "First exhibition in France at the Pavillon de Marsan in the Louvre. Tériade publishes *Les Européens* (cover illustrated by Miró)." ICP (verbatim): "He received the Overseas Press Club Award four times; the American Society of Magazine Photographers award in 1953; and the Prix de la Société Française de Photographie in 1959, among other honors." The 1954 USSR access is the single most-cited diplomatic-photography achievement in Cartier-Bresson's mid-1950s career — Stalin died March 1953, and Cartier-Bresson's USSR access marks the formal post-Stalin opening of Western photojournalism into Soviet space. The 1954 Delpire publications *Les Danses à Bali* and *D'une Chine à l'autre* are the published Far-East-period books most directly contemporaneous with the FoM Asian plates; **no source consulted this round identifies whether any FoM Bali, Indonesia, China, Kashmir, Iran, or Java plate also appears in those Delpire publications.** The 1955 Pavillon de Marsan exhibition opened during the same year as *The Family of Man* — neither the Fondation nor any source consulted this round connects the two shows.

**Post-1955 (out of the FoM run-up, for completeness).** Fondation entries (verbatim, live fetch this session): "1958: Returns to China for three months for the tenth anniversary of the People's Republic of China." "1963: Returns to Mexico for the second time. *Life* magazine sends him to Cuba." "1974: Terminates his active working relationship with Magnum Photos, although the agency distribution retains his archives. Concentrates on drawing." ICP (verbatim): "In 1966 he left Magnum, which remained his agent, and devoted himself to drawing." The ICP and Fondation pages diverge on the year Cartier-Bresson left Magnum's active activity — ICP says 1966, Fondation 1974 (or "From 1968, he began to curtail his photographic activities" per the Magnum profile). Magnum and Fondation place the formal termination of active work later; ICP places it earlier. Discrepancy preserved, not adjudicated. The 2000 founding of the Fondation Henri Cartier-Bresson (with Martine Franck and Mélanie) and Cartier-Bresson's death on 3 August 2004 in Montjustin, Provence are anchored verbatim on the Fondation 2000 and 2004 entries.

## Reception of Cartier-Bresson's FoM contribution in the critical literature

Cartier-Bresson's individual presence is rarely the explicit subject of the critical literature on FoM; what *is* discussed at length is the universalising frame that Cartier-Bresson's eleven-plate, eight-country, decisive-moment aesthetic exemplifies. The four major monographs in repo (Sandeen, Stimson, Turner, Phillips) all have **body text NOT consulted in this or prior rounds** per the in-repo source files — only metadata, table-of-contents, and argument-summaries are recorded. They are cited below at the level the source files actually support, with that limitation stated explicitly, mirroring the Smith and Lange files. The one critical source with verbatim content re-read this session is Barthes.

**Curatorial / institutional reading.** The MoMA archives summary (`src-moma-archives-highlights-1955`, Tier-1, in-repo, re-read this session) presents the narrative arc as "entrance archway with crowd imagery → lovers → childbirth → household life → careers → death → H-bomb → return to children / new life, closing on W. Eugene Smith's *A Walk to Paradise Garden* (1946)" (verbatim from the source file). Cartier-Bresson's plates sit across the entire arc: #31 in Marriage (childbirth/lovers register), #128 and #190 in Land/Work, #259 in Food (everyday), #288 in Relationships, #366 and #371 in Religious Expression, #409 in Revolt (the closest the exhibition gets to naming explicit political conflict), #442 in Government, #481 and #483 in Childhood Magic (the terminal renewal sequence). The exhibition's curatorial frame deploys Cartier-Bresson's geographic breadth to populate the universal-human categories with internationally-sourced documentary evidence — precisely the move the critical tradition attacks.

**Barthes (critical / theoretical, anchored at quote-level).** Roland Barthes's "The Great Family of Man" (*Mythologies*, 1957; `src-barthes-1957`, Tier-2, in-repo, re-read this session) is the foundational counter-reading. Barthes's "myth functions in two stages" formulation (verbatim, re-read this session): *"first the difference between human morphologies is asserted, exoticism is insistently stressed … Then, from this pluralism, a type of unity is magically produced: man is born, works, laughs and dies everywhere in the same way."* The Cartier-Bresson cluster is an exemplary case of the "first stage": France, Bali, Kashmir, China, Iran, Java, Spain — a catalogue of cultural difference, each location named and visually distinguished. The "second stage" — the magical production of unity — is the curatorial work the exhibition performs by placing these plates in universal-human categories ("Marriage," "Land," "Food," "Childhood Magic"). Barthes does not name Cartier-Bresson or any specific plate in the excerpts in repo; his critique applies at the level of the show's overall logic, but it applies with particular force to the eleven-plate, eight-country spread of the single most-represented foreign-territory photographer in the show. Barthes's closing line (verbatim, re-read this session) — *"So that I rather fear that the final justification of all this Adamism is to give to the immobility of the world the alibi of a 'wisdom' and a 'lyricism' which only make the gestures of man look eternal the better to defuse them"* — bears directly on the "decisive moment" doctrine: the moment in which "the world falls into apparent order and meaning" (Cartier-Bresson's own phrase, per the ICP page re-read this session) is structurally akin to the moment in which Barthes's Adamism makes "the gestures of man look eternal." That structural parallel is documented in the photographic-theory literature (Sekula, *Photography Against the Grain*, named but not page-cited in repo; **NOT consulted in this round**) but the Barthes essay itself does not draw the parallel by name.

**Aperture 1955 — the contemporaneous peer-community debate.** *Aperture* 3, no. 2 (1955) ran a thematic issue titled "The Controversial 'Family of Man'" (`src-aperture-1955-controversial`, Tier-2, in-repo, re-read this session, `verified: false`). The source file records Barbara Morgan's "The Theme Show: A Contemporary Exhibition Technique" and Cora Alsberg / George Wright's "One Family's Opinion" as the two named contributions; Alsberg / Wright raised the concern that the mixed-photographer display caused photographers to "surrender their individuality" (verbatim from the source file, re-read this session). Cartier-Bresson — with eleven plates distributed across nine sections, all credited to the Magnum cooperative agency rather than to him individually as the print-maker — would be one of the most exposed photographers to the "surrender their individuality" critique: his decisive-moment authorship is subsumed into the sequenced thematic argument of the curator. **Interior text of the *Aperture* issue was not consulted in any round** (archive.org print-disabled; body text not retrieved); whether Cartier-Bresson is named in the issue, or whether he responded to it, is not stated in any source consulted. No claim that the *Aperture* critique was directed at Cartier-Bresson by name is made here.

**Sandeen, Stimson, Turner, Phillips.** *Picturing an Exhibition: The Family of Man and 1950s America* (Sandeen, 1995; `src-sandeen-1995`, Tier-2, in-repo), *The Pivot of the World* (Stimson, 2006; `src-stimson-2006`, Tier-2, in-repo), *The Democratic Surround* (Turner, 2013; `src-turner-2013`, Tier-2, in-repo), and *The Judgment Seat of Photography* (Phillips, 1982; `src-phillips-1982-judgment-seat`, Tier-2, in-repo) are the standard book-length and journal-article critical references. **For all four, the in-repo source files record body text NOT consulted** — metadata, table-of-contents, and argument-summaries only. Sandeen's confirmed chapter titles include "The family of man on the move" and "The family of man in Moscow"; Turner's confirmed chapter "The museum of modern art makes the world a family" addresses the exhibition directly. Phillips returned JSTOR HTTP 403; no body text accessed. The argument-summary level of each is sufficient to record their orientation — Sandeen historical-contextual, Stimson on photography-and-nationalism, Turner on Cold War liberal-internationalist multimedia, Phillips on MoMA's institutional ideology — but no page-level Cartier-Bresson-specific claim from any of the four is citable here. The Phillips argument summary (carried from the task brief, not from a primary fetch) that Steichen's exhibitions instrumentalised photography for Cold War liberal ends would, if confirmed page-level, bear directly on the question of whether Cartier-Bresson's decisive-moment aesthetic was enrolled in a Cold War cultural-diplomacy project — but the body text not consulted means the claim is structural, not page-cited.

**The Szarkowski 1964/1966 canonisation point.** The `src-szarkowski-1964-photographers-eye-reception` source (Tier-3, in-repo, re-read this session, `verified: false`) records that the MoMA Store product page (fetched in that prior round 2026-05-17) describes Szarkowski's *The Photographer's Eye* book as containing "172 photographs spanning photography's entire history, including celebrated work by Henri Cartier-Bresson, Walker Evans, Edward Steichen, Paul Strand, and Edward Weston, alongside vernacular documents and amateur snapshots" — the MoMA Store description names Cartier-Bresson first in its illustrative roster. This is the institutional canonisation point: Cartier-Bresson is named at the head of the modernist roster of the post-FoM curatorial paradigm at MoMA. The 1955 FoM and 1964/1966 *Photographer's Eye* both feature Cartier-Bresson centrally but in opposing curatorial frames — humanist-universalist in 1955, formalist-autonomous in 1964/1966. That structural fact is documentable from in-repo sources; no individual reading of Cartier-Bresson's role in either show is anchored at page level here.

**The "decisive moment vs. curated sequence" framing — both readings, neither endorsed.** Cartier-Bresson's published theoretical position (the "decisive moment" as the unit of photographic meaning — author-controlled, anchored on the photographer's intuitive recognition of order in the world) sits in latent tension with Steichen's curatorial method (which assembled individual decisive moments into a sequenced thematic argument the photographer did not control). The redemptive-arc reading — Cartier-Bresson's plates among the documentary anchors for a universal-human story, with the closing Childhood Magic placements (#481 Java and #483 Spain) participating in the terminal renewal sequence — is the institutional perspective. The critical tradition (Barthes anchored at quote-level; Phillips/Sandeen/Stimson/Turner named but not page-cited; the "surrender of individuality" framing of the 1955 *Aperture* peer debate at title-level only) treats precisely the curated-sequence move as the depoliticisation work the exhibition performs. **This file does not endorse either reading as settled.** Both are documented; neither is presented as the resolved interpretation, consistent with the hedge in `research/photographs/photo-0028.md`, `research/photographs/photo-0122.md`, and `research/photographs/photo-0395.md`, and with the Smith and Lange files' parallel structure.
