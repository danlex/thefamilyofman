# Dorothea Lange (1895–1965)

Dorothea Lange was an American documentary photographer whose Depression-era work for the Farm Security Administration produced some of the defining images of twentieth-century photojournalism. The ICP archive page (fetched 2026-05-07) records that she was "Born in Hoboken, New Jersey" and was "training as a teacher when she decided to become a professional photographer in 1913" (src-icp-lange-archive). The Wikipedia pointer (fetched 2026-05-07) gives the day-month resolution as May 26, 1895 in Hoboken and October 11, 1965 in San Francisco (src-wikipedia-lange-pointer).

## Career

Lange "worked at the studio of the Pictorialist photographer Arnold Genthe in 1914 and studied at the Clarence H. White School in 1917" before moving to San Francisco, "where she opened a portrait studio which she operated from 1919 to 1940" (src-icp-lange-archive). The shift from studio portraiture to social-documentary work is dated on the ICP page to 1929: "she began to photograph people in the context of their daily lives." She married Paul Taylor (described in `src-icp-lange-archive` as "an economist at Berkeley") in 1935 and "collaborated on the book *An American Exodus* in 1939" (src-icp-lange-archive).

The ICP page anchors her FSA period explicitly: "Between 1935 and 1939, Lange traveled extensively for the Farm Security Administration, for which she made many of her best-known photographs, including *Migrant Mother*" (src-icp-lange-archive). She received "the first Guggenheim Fellowship awarded to a woman in 1941" (src-icp-lange-archive). Between 1942 and 1945 she "worked for the U.S. government photographing such subjects such as the Japanese-American internment camps" (the WebFetch summary returned this with the "such as" doubled — preserved verbatim per src-icp-lange-archive). The ICP page closes by noting that, "Diagnosed with terminal cancer in 1965, she devoted herself to preparing for a retrospective exhibition at the Museum of Modern Art, held posthumously in 1966" (src-icp-lange-archive).

## Connection to *The Family of Man*

Lange is represented by nine plates in *The Family of Man* (count verified by strict-match grep against `data/photographs.csv` 2026-05-07): photo-0045 (Section 7 Births), photo-0087 (Section 11 Children B), photo-0093 (Section 11 Children B, with explicit Farm Security Administration commissioner credit), photo-0127 (Section 14 Land), photo-0175 (Section 16 Work B), photo-0286 (Section 25 Relationships, FORTUNE), photo-0291 (Section 25 Relationships, FORTUNE), photo-0376 (Section 31 Hard Times, FSA), and photo-0379 (Section 31 Hard Times, FSA) — all credited "American" in the MoMA Master Checklist (src-moma-exh-0569-master-checklist). Three of the nine plates carry an explicit FSA commissioner credit, which dates them to her 1935–1939 federal documentary work. The two FORTUNE-credited plates in Section 25 reflect her later magazine assignments (FORTUNE was an early publisher of Lange's work — the specific assignment dating is not anchored to a fetched source this round).

The ICP page does not mention *The Family of Man* explicitly; the connection is anchored at the plate level via the MoMA Master Checklist. Lange's nine-plate presence places her among the most heavily represented American photographers in the exhibition. Strict-match grep against `data/photographs.csv` (2026-05-07) gives the leaders' counts as Wayne Miller 12, Homer Page 10, Lange 9 — making Lange the fourth-largest individual contributor (Eisenstaedt also has 7, Bourke-White 6) and one of the top American documentary voices in the show.

The Daily Art Magazine 70th-anniversary article (src-west-2025-dailyart-lange-fom, in repo, `verified: false`) further argues that beyond the nine plates, Lange played a curatorial role in helping Steichen shape the project — but that claim was not consulted in this round at the article's full text and is preserved as a pointer for future verification.
