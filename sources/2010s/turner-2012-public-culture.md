## Tier justification

Tier 2: peer-reviewed academic article in *Public Culture*, a Duke University Press journal listed in `CREDIBILITY.md` § Tier-2 ("peer-reviewed journals: ... *Public Culture* (Duke UP)..."). Author Fred Turner is Harry and Norman Chandler Professor of Communication at Stanford University and the author of the Tier-2 monograph `The Democratic Surround` (University of Chicago Press, 2013), which builds on this article. The article is the original peer-reviewed venue for what would become *The Democratic Surround*'s lead chapter on *The Family of Man*.

## Relevance

This is the Tier-2 anchor that prior rounds of this project flagged as "Turner 2013 — widely cited as the strongest recent reading of the exhibition's liberal-internationalist visual culture. **Not in this repo, not fetched.**" (`research/world-tour.md` §7). Carries Tier-2 anchors for several headline tour figures, USIA framing, and the liberal-internationalist counter-reading of the Barthes / Sekula / Solomon-Godeau critique.

## Key excerpts / pages

Direct fetch 2026-05-09 (saved at `.scratch/turner-family-of-man-pc-24.1.pdf`, ~734 KB; Stanford-hosted preprint of the published *Public Culture* article).

- **Citation block** (footer, pp. 55–84): "*Public Culture* 24:1 · DOI 10.1215/08992363-1443556 · Copyright 2012 by Duke University Press"

- **Title and author** (p. 55): "*The Family of Man and the Politics of Attention in Cold War America* · Fred Turner"

- **Opening attendance / new-york framing** (p. 55): "In the early spring of 1955, more than a quarter million people streamed through the doors of the Museum of Modern Art in New York. […] *The Family of Man* filled the entire second floor of the museum. A series of temporary walls designed by architect Paul Rudolph channeled visitors through the images, allowing them to move at their own pace […]"

- **Tour aggregate, 37 countries / 7.5 million / 5 million catalogues** (p. 56): "In the wake of its run at the Museum of Modern Art, copies of the show traveled around the United States and, thanks to funding from the United States Information Agency (USIA), to thirty-seven foreign countries as well. The USIA estimates that more than 7.5 million visitors saw the exhibition abroad in the ten years after it opened in New York.[1] By 1978 the exhibition catalog had sold more than 5 million copies, and it remains in print today. Since 1994 the exhibition has even enjoyed a permanent home in a castle in Clervaux, Luxembourg."

- **Footnote 1 to Szarkowski 1994** (p. 56): "1. John Szarkowski, 'The Family of Man,' in John Szarkowski and Museum of Modern Art (New York), *The Museum of Modern Art at Mid-century at Home and Abroad* (New York: Museum of Modern Art, 1994), 13."

- **Critical framing** (p. 56): "In the decades since it first appeared, however, *The Family of Man* has also become a whipping boy for middlebrow midcentury aesthetics and for an oppressive view of the public it ostensibly encoded. Since the early 1970s, critics have attacked the show as a species of American mythology (Roland Barthes), an attempt to paper over problems of race and class (Christopher Phillips, John Berger, and Abigail Solomon-Godeau), and even an act of aesthetic colonialism (Allan Sekula).[2]"

- **Footnote 2 — bibliography of the critical literature** (p. 56): cites Barthes, "La grande famille des hommes" *Mythologies* (1957); Christopher Phillips, "The Judgment Seat of Photography," *October* 22 (1982); John Berger, *About Looking* (1980); Abigail Solomon-Godeau in the 2004 Schmidt-Linsenhoff *Family of Man, 1955–2001* edited volume; Allan Sekula, "The Traffic in Photographs," *Art Journal* 41, no. 1 (1981): 15–21; Monique Berlier's "Readings of an Exhibition" chapter in Brennen and Hardt eds. *Picturing the Past* (1999); Sandeen 1995; Stimson 2006.

- **Counter-claim** (p. 57): "Without for a moment denying *The Family of Man*'s middlebrow aesthetics or the facts of racial, sexual, and political repression in 1950s America, I want to scrape away several decades' worth of critical disdain and illuminate a deeply democratic, even utopian, impulse that drove the show and much of the early audience response to it. […] Steichen and his audience saw the show as something very different. At the start of World War II and again at the start of the Cold War, the intellectuals, artists, and policy makers Steichen traveled among saw the rise of authoritarianism as a simultaneously social and psychological problem."

## Notes

- Direct fetch 2026-05-09. The Stanford-hosted PDF (Turner's faculty preprint) at `https://fredturner.stanford.edu/sites/g/files/sbiybj27111/files/media/file/turner-family-of-man-pc-24.1.pdf` returned HTTP 200 to curl. The published version has a Duke UP DOI and is paywalled at the *Public Culture* journal landing; the preprint and published version are textually identical for the passages cited above.
- **What this anchors at Tier-2**: "thirty-seven foreign countries" tour-scope figure (this is the load-bearing Tier-2 anchor for the headline "37 countries" claim that appears throughout the tour-summary literature, including in CNA materials); "more than 7.5 million visitors" tour-aggregate attendance figure with USIA attribution (cited to Szarkowski 1994). This is a *different attendance figure* from the CNA "10 million" figure and from the O'Brian 2008 "9 million in 61 countries" figure — all three are Tier-2 attestations and all three disagree.
- **Counter-reading anchor**: Turner explicitly positions himself against the Barthes / Berger / Sekula / Solomon-Godeau critique. His liberal-democratic / "antiauthoritarian visual culture" reading is the Tier-2 alternative to the Tier-2 critical readings carried by `src-barthes-1957-fr` (in repo, body text not fetched). For the world-tour discussion specifically, Turner's reading frames the USIA tour as continuous with FDR-era democratic visual culture, not (only) as Cold War propaganda.
- **What this does NOT anchor**: per-venue, per-city, or per-country tour data. Turner cites the aggregate figure ("thirty-seven foreign countries") but does not enumerate them; the article's research focus is the New York installation and the show's American intellectual context (Margaret Mead, Ruth Benedict, John Dewey, Paul Rudolph, Herbert Bayer, Cornell Capa, the post-Bauhaus Aspen / Black Mountain network), not the international itinerary.
- **Source of the "37 countries" figure**: Turner cites Szarkowski 1994 (footnote 1), not NARA RG 306 directly. Szarkowski 1994 is itself a MoMA publication (1994 Mid-Century at Home and Abroad volume) — Tier-1 institutional. The chain is therefore: Turner 2012 (Tier-2 article) → Szarkowski 1994 (Tier-1 MoMA volume, NOT consulted in this round) → presumably USIA reporting. The "37 countries" figure is anchored at Tier-2 but its primary source remains one step away. Promote to Tier-1 only if Szarkowski 1994 is opened directly.
- **2013 book version**: Turner's article was substantially expanded into Chapter 5 of *The Democratic Surround: Multimedia and American Liberalism from World War II to the Psychedelic Sixties* (University of Chicago Press, 2013). The book version is the canonical reference in the secondary literature; it is not in this repo and was not fetched in this round. The 2012 article is the peer-reviewed-journal version; for cite-purposes the article is acceptable substitute.
- **Scope of anti-confabulation flag**: Turner 2012 supplies the "37 countries" figure and the "7.5 million" figure at Tier-2. These can be added to `research/world-tour.md` as Tier-2 anchors. The "9 million" figure carried in some in-repo wiki pages with `verified: false` is NOT anchored by Turner 2012 — Turner gives 7.5 million; the 9-10 million figures are in CNA materials (Tier-1) and O'Brian 2008 (Tier-2). Document the disagreement honestly.
